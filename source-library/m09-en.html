From link position to the physics of motion

M9 • 03 Jun 2027 - 06 Jul 2027 • 75 hours

What you will produce by the end of the month
The deliverable for this month is a joint model using the parameters identified in M7, together with
a dynamics simulation of a two-link mechanism or leg. Check dimensions, the energy balance and
agreement between calculations and measurements. The kinematics from M8 finds the angles that
place a link endpoint at a specified point; dynamics relates motion to the required torque and
actuator limits. For a leg, it describes the effects of link masses, body motion and foot contact; for a
finger, it describes phalange acceleration, load holding and the reaction when touching an object.
Begin with one link, then add a second link and contact.

Inputs
Bring the geometry, coordinate frames, FK and Jacobians from M8. From M7, bring angle, command
and current measurements and the available actuator model, including friction, delay and the
limits of identification. From M5, bring CAD-part masses and centres of mass, provided they were
determined correctly. Label each quantity as measured, calculated in CAD, taken from
manufacturer documentation or specified for an illustrative example. Inertia calculated in CAD
using a default density is not the measured inertia of a printed part. Account for fasteners, wiring
and actual infill; if data are unavailable, state the uncertainty of the estimate.
Initial self-check: distinguish kg from N, N from N·m, velocity from acceleration, and inertia about
the centre of mass from inertia about the joint axis. Check the work done by a force through a
small displacement and calculate the energy of a load at a known height. Make sure you know the
axis from which q is measured and the direction of gravity. An incorrect potential-energy sign can
produce smooth simulated motion that contradicts physics.

Scope and evidence
M9 does not require the dynamics of a complete multi-legged platform with gait control. The basic
model has a fixed base, two links and explicitly selected contacts. Increase complexity only after
checking simple limiting cases. Simulation can confirm the correctness of the equations and
numerical method, but agreement with a real actuator requires comparison against a separate
measured run. If M7 did not produce physical measurements, complete the computational work
and mark the comparison with the real actuator as unfinished.
Prepare a working integrator and a description of the model’s limits of applicability. State the
angle, velocity and load ranges and contact modes that you have checked. Separately describe the
conditions under which backlash, saturation, compliance, heating or an unknown internal actuator
controller become significant. Subsequent modules will use this information to choose control laws
that account for model accuracy.

75 hours: from simple models to complex
ones

Four blocks
Hours 1-20: Newton’s and Euler’s equations, mass, centre of mass, inertia, forces and energy; 10
hours of theory and 10 hours of practice. Hours 21-40: Lagrange’s equations, equation structure
and two-link dynamics; 10+10. Hours 41-60: normal contact, friction, impact and static support;
10+10. Hours 61-75: use of the M7 identification results, model validation, sensitivity and error
assessment; 5+10. This totals 35 hours of study and analysis and 40 hours of implementation and
experiments. The allocation includes documenting results rather than leaving the report to
uncounted time.
Divide each of the first three 10-hour theory blocks into 2-hour sessions: reading a section, deriving
equations by hand, checking units and preparing an experiment. Schedule the 10-hour practical
blocks on Sundays, allowing breaks between stages. Split the final 5 hours of theory as 2+2+1. The
module dates establish its place in the overall calendar; retain the boundary dates, rest days,
holidays and separate integration checks. Hour numbers express the workload: do not count the
same time twice when placing sessions in the calendar.

The dynamics-lab project structure
parameters/ contains masses, lengths, centres of mass, inertias and their provenance. models/
contains the single-link, 2R-mechanism and contact models. experiments/ stores inputs, initial
states and integration settings. data/measured/ preserves the M7 data unchanged; data/simulated/
stores computational results. validation/ contains comparison results and runs for independent
validation; reports/ contains energy balances and model limits. Each simulation records the
equation version, solver step size or tolerances, duration, input signals and termination reason.

When to increase complexity
First, check the energy of one link without friction or external input. Second, add a known torque
and compare its work with the energy change. Third, introduce viscous friction and check that
energy decreases. Fourth, repeat these checks for two links. Fifth, add contact and account for
energy losses. Sixth, use the real actuator model from M7 and an independent measurement
recording. If a basic check fails, find the cause first; do not try to remove the discrepancy by
increasing damping or fitting a physical parameter.
Separate measurement uncertainty from numerical error. Changing mass explores sensitivity to a
physical parameter; reducing the integrator step explores numerical error. Include both
experiments in the report; one cannot replace the other. At the outset, choose several simple
motions that you can explain without a computer, and revisit them after adding each new equation
term.

Newton and Euler: forces, torques and inertia

How to draw a force and torque diagram
For translation of the centre of mass, the sum of external forces equals m·a_COM. For planar
rotation of a rigid link about a fixed axis, sum torques about that axis and use the moment of
inertia about the same axis. In general spatial motion, the rotational equation includes I·ω̇ and the
gyroscopic term ω×(Iω), expressed in a consistent basis. Do not automatically apply the planar
relation τ=Iα to an arbitrary rotating system. The centre of mass is the point whose motion
describes the translational part of the body’s motion; the mass distribution relative to the axes
determines its inertia.

Task 1. Physical parameters of a link
The inputs are the M5 CAD link and available mass measurements. 1. Separate geometry from
material and list the contributions of fasteners and the actuator. 2. Determine mass, COM position
in the local coordinate frame and inertia about the COM. 3. Transfer the inertia to the joint axis
using the parallel-axis theorem; in the planar case, I_joint=I_COM+m·d². 4. Compare with a rod or
point-mass approximation. Deliverable: a parameter table with units and sources. Check: shifting
the axis away from the COM increases inertia by a non-negative amount. Edge case: an incorrect
CAD scale changes inertia more strongly than it changes a linear dimension.

Task 2. Forces and torques on a leg or finger link
Draw one link showing its weight, joint reaction force, actuator torque and an external force at its
endpoint. 1. Choose the point about which you calculate torques. 2. Write down lever arms and
signs. 3. Calculate the holding torque in several known configurations. 4. Check the external-force
torque using τ=JᵀF from M8; calculate the gravitational contribution separately. Deliverable: three
labelled drawings and a torque table. Check: if a force’s line of action passes through the axis, its
torque about that axis is zero. Error to avoid: a link’s mass cannot be added as a force without
multiplying by g.

Energy and units
A single link’s kinetic energy is the sum of 0.5m·v_COM² and 0.5ωᵀI_COMω. Potential energy in a
uniform gravitational field depends on COM height. For two links, add their energies; obtain COM
velocities from the geometry and Jacobians in M8. Prepare a table: length m, mass kg, time s, force
N, torque N·m, energy J, inertia kg·m². Check the dimensions of every term before running a
numerical calculation. Using I for both inertia and electric current in different files is acceptable
only when the context is clear; separate names such as inertia and current are preferable.

One link: its equation and an energy test

Task 3. A single-joint pendulum
For clarity, measure θ from the downward vertical. With moment of inertia J about the axis, mass m
and distance c to the COM, a simple model is J·θ̈+b·θ̇+m·g·c·sin(θ)=τ. A convenient zero for
potential energy is the lowest point: V=m·g·c·(1-cosθ). If you use an angle q measured from the
horizontal axis, the gravitational term changes; do not switch conventions without transforming the
coordinates. 1. Derive the gravitational torque from a drawing. 2. Write the state [θ,ω] and its
derivative [ω,α]. 3. Solve the equation for α, then integrate. 4. Compare the initial acceleration
direction with a hand calculation.
Deliverable: angle, velocity and energy plots, plus a configuration that reproduces the calculation.
Check without friction or torque: total energy should be conserved within numerical error, which
you investigate by reducing the step size or tolerances. For a small angle, compare the period with
2π·sqrt(J/(m·g·c)) for positive parameters. Edge cases: the lowest point with zero initial velocity
remains an equilibrium; reject negative mass or zero inertia as an invalid model.

Task 4. Work and dissipation
First disable gravity and apply a constant torque to a link with known inertia J: acceleration should
be τ/J. Then restore gravity and apply a bounded torque pulse. Numerically integrate power P=τ·ω
and compare work with the energy change. Add viscous friction b·ω with b≥0: the dissipated power
b·ω² is non-negative. Save the balance E(t)-E(0)-actuator_work+losses, as well as the q plot. Check:
with zero actuator torque and positive damping, mechanical energy must not systematically
increase. Negative b is a separate artificial test that the validator must reject in a passive model.

The numerical method is part of the experiment
Compare fixed-step integration with the adaptive solve_ivp solver. Record duration, step size,
relative and absolute tolerances, and termination status. Run an experiment with successive
step-size reductions while keeping physical parameters unchanged. Assess both the trajectory and
the energy-balance residual. Similar position plots do not yet establish convergence of force or
acceleration. High velocities and stiff contact will change the integrator requirements; preserve this
basic model without contact for subsequent comparisons.

Lagrange’s equations and the structure of
two-link dynamics

What to derive
The generalised coordinates q=[q1,q2] uniquely specify the 2R configuration. Measure q1 from the
horizontal x-axis and q2 relative to the first link; use the x-y plane with y upward. Construct the
centre-of-mass positions, differentiate them with respect to time, and calculate kinetic energy K
and potential energy V. The Lagrangian is L=K-V. For each q_i, write d/dt(∂L/∂q̇_i)-∂L/∂q_i=τ_i,
adding external forces and losses separately. A symbolic algebra system can help with derivatives,
but you must be able to explain every energy expression and the choice of signs.

Task 5. Mass matrix and gravity
Use l1,l2 for lengths, c1,c2 for distances from the corresponding joints to the COMs, m1,m2 for
masses, and I1,I2 for planar inertias about the COMs. Extract the structure M(q)·q̈+h(q,q̇)+g(q)=τ
from the equations. For checking: M22=I2+m2·c2², M12=M21=I2+m2·(c2²+l1·c2·cosq2),
M11=I1+I2+m1·c1²+m2·(l1²+c2²+2l1·c2·cosq2). Here h combines the velocity-dependent terms;
an individual matrix C is not unique, so check the vector h first.
1. Obtain M and g from your own derivation. 2. Compare M against an independent implementation
or the structure given above. 3. Check symmetry and positive definiteness in admissible
configurations. 4. Compare gravitational torque with the numerical derivative of V with respect to
q. Deliverable: a computational notebook with the derivation, a numerical function and tests. For
the chosen coordinates, g2=m2·g·c2·cos(q1+q2), while g1 additionally contains
g·(m1·c1+m2·l1)·cosq1. Edge case: all h terms must vanish when q̇=0.

Task 6. Forward and inverse dynamics
The inverse problem takes q,q̇,q̈ and returns the required τ; the forward problem takes q,q̇,τ and
solves a linear system for q̈. 1. Choose several states. 2. Calculate τ using inverse dynamics. 3.
Feed it into forward dynamics and compare the reconstructed acceleration. 4. Repeat with
endpoint forces added through JᵀF in a consistent basis. Save the acceleration reconstruction errors
after the inverse-then-forward calculation. Use solve(M,rhs), not an explicit matrix inverse. This test
does not replace an independent physical check: both functions may contain the same model error.

2R simulation and checks of physical
behaviour

Task 7. Two links without contact
Inputs: validated M,h,g, the M8 geometry, and physically admissible masses and inertias. 1. Run
free motion from a non-zero initial configuration without friction. 2. Plot angles, velocities, the
endpoint path and total energy. 3. Repeat with half the step size or tighter tolerances. 4. Add
damping and bounded torque, checking the work balance. Deliverable: four comparable runs and a
numerical-convergence report. Edge case: with horizontal links and no holding torque, acceleration
must not be zero merely because the initial velocities are zero.

Checking velocity-dependent terms
For the 2R model described above, define a=m2·l1·c2·sin(q2). Then h1=-a·(2q̇1q̇2+q̇2²), h2=a·q̇1².
Compare this with your own derivation and check reversal of velocity direction. Do not remove h
simply because calculations agree with an experiment at low velocity: the error will change as
velocities change. However, a slow-motion model may deliberately neglect some dynamics if this is
an explicit approximation and its error has been checked. Record the velocity range in which the
approximation remains useful.

Application to a leg and hand
Use the 2R model as a planar section of a leg or as two phalanges. For the leg, add the masses of
the relevant parts and actuators mounted on the links; for the finger, specify a different length
scale and fingertip load. Compare gravitational torque and inertial effects for the same normalised
motion. Do not transfer planar-model equations to a complete spatial leg without a new derivation:
the first axis, COM offsets and inertia tensors change the equations. It is sufficient to specify the
interface through which a multibody library will later connect.

An independent numerical reference
Build the same simple mechanism in one tool, such as Drake, MuJoCo or Featherstone’s reference
library. First disable contact and default actuators, and align gravity, masses, COMs, inertias and
initial coordinates. Compare acceleration in one pose before comparing a long trajectory; then
compare energies and a short motion. Disagreement may result from built-in damping, limit stops
or a different coordinate frame. In the comparison report, list the effects that are enabled and
disabled. You need not install several heavyweight simulators for one plot: one independent
reference is enough.

Contact, friction and body support

Task 8. Unilateral normal contact
A horizontal surface can exert an upward force on a foot, but cannot attract it towards the floor.
Begin with a vertical mass and a spring-damper model of penetration δ≥0. An illustrative model
force N=max(0,k·δ+c·δ̇) acts only during contact; δ̇ is positive when penetration increases. 1.
Check free fall before touchdown. 2. Add contact and measure maximum penetration. 3. Check the
force sign and absence of tension during lift-off. 4. Compare several k,c values and integrator step
sizes. Deliverable: height, force, energy and the region of numerical stability. These parameters do
not automatically represent the properties of a real foot.

Task 9. Sticking and sliding
In a simple Coulomb description, static tangential force is bounded by |F_t|≤μ_sN; during sliding it
opposes velocity, with a magnitude determined by μ_kN. 1. Place a body on a horizontal plane. 2.
Gradually increase the prescribed horizontal force. 3. Show the transition from sticking to sliding. 4.
Repeat with a different normal load. Deliverable: plots of tangential force, velocity and contact
mode. Check: tangential friction must not systematically add mechanical energy. Edge case N=0:
the contact friction force also vanishes. Smoothing near zero velocity changes the model and must
be explicitly identified.

Impact and discrete time
Compare elastic and inelastic impact with a continuous compliant model. The coefficient of
restitution describes the ratio of normal velocities after and before impact under specified
conditions; it is not a universal material coefficient for every velocity. Do not combine a rigid
impulsive impact with the same energy loss in a damper without checking for double counting. For
the first implementation, explain the two approaches and implement one, then investigate
sensitivity. Force, penetration and energy matter in contact; smooth animation can hide
non-physical high-frequency oscillations.

Task 10. Support polygon
Take known contact points on a horizontal plane and the projection of the body COM. Construct the
convex hull of active contacts and the signed distance from the projection to its boundary: positive
inside and negative outside. Repeat after removing one support and shifting the COM. Deliverable:
support diagrams and the static margin in metres. Check: a point on an edge has zero margin; two
contacts do not form a polygon with non-zero area. This is a quasi-static geometric check under
stated force and friction assumptions, not proof of the dynamic stability of a walking robot.

Validating the M7 model and assessing its
accuracy

Task 11. A joint with real actuator parameters
First establish what M7 identified: mechanics under a known torque, or the response of a servo with
an internal controller to a position command. 1. Import parameters with units, signs and operating
ranges. 2. Check gearing and inertia: do not count the load twice. 3. Replay the measured input in
simulation with the same initial state. 4. Compare q(t) and the available velocities and currents on
a common time axis. Deliverable: overlaid plots, a residual and an explanation of the most
prominent discrepancy. Do not connect a position-command response model as an ideal torque
source: these are different physical interfaces.
If torque is not directly known, validate the available command-to-angle model and state
separately which mechanical properties remain unconfirmed. Current telemetry can support an
explanation of saturation, but does not become an accurate torque measurement without a
coefficient and a definition of the current type. Add heating, friction, backlash and delay from M7
only where their meaning is consistent with the new equation. For each effect, first show a
comparison against the model without it; otherwise you cannot determine what improved the
result.

Task 12. An independent recording and sensitivity
Before fitting parameters, set aside recordings of separate runs for independent validation. 1. Fit
the model on the first group. 2. Freeze the parameters. 3. Predict a new run with a different
admissible command waveform or load. 4. Calculate angle RMSE, maximum residual and
characteristic-time error. Then vary one parameter at a time within its justified uncertainty range
and compare the effects. Deliverable: a sensitivity table and independent-validation plots. If no
new real data are available, perform a software test, but leave physical validation open.

Assessing model errors
Prepare a table of error source, observed effect, required accuracy and refinement method. For
example: COM affects gravitational torque; J affects acceleration; delay shifts the response; friction
affects low velocities; contact k affects penetration and peak force. Allocate time to the parameters
that most strongly affect the result of interest. State numerical integration error and measurement
noise separately. The aim is the smallest model adequate for the chosen experiment, rather than
the largest number of parameters. The report must identify admissible operating conditions and
specific examples where the model ceases to be reliable.

Acceptance checks: detecting physical errors

Test scenarios
F1: no gravity, friction or external torque. Rest is maintained; with an initial velocity, the
corresponding energy balance holds. F2: one link under constant torque without gravity.
Acceleration agrees with τ/J. F3: positive viscous friction without actuation. Energy decreases
within integration error. F4: geometry is scaled incorrectly. The unit table and comparison against
the analytical period reveal the error; visual similarity of the trajectory alone is insufficient to
accept the simulation. F5: the mass matrix is asymmetric or not positive definite for a physical
configuration. The test rejects the model before integration.
F6: contact without penetration and during lift-off. No attractive normal force occurs. F7: tangential
sliding. Frictional work does not create energy; contact force vanishes when N=0. F8: integration
step size is reduced without changing physical parameters. The result must show convergence of
the metrics of interest; otherwise conclusions about mechanics are premature. F9: the COM moves
outside the horizontal static support polygon. The margin changes sign; the test does not
automatically conclude that dynamic falling occurs. F10: parameters are fitted to one run, but do
not reproduce a new one. Narrow the validity region or improve the model and repeat independent
validation.

Evidence that the module is complete
Provide dimensional and energy tests; checks of M,h,g; input parameters with provenance; a
two-link or leg simulation; contact sensitivity analysis; transfer of the M7 identification results; and
comparison of a measured trend with calculation. The measured trend may be delay, characteristic
time, load dependence or decay; choose the metric in advance. The report must explicitly identify
conditions under which the model fails. Without a physical recording, the corresponding item
remains open even if every mathematical test passes.

Questions and handover to the next module
Explain why moment of inertia depends on the axis; where velocity-dependent terms come from;
how to check the gravity sign; why to solve a system with M rather than calculate its inverse; why
excessively stiff contact complicates integration; how the friction cone differs from the static
support-polygon rule; why a servo model with an internal controller cannot be treated as an ideal
torque actuator; and how to distinguish noise, numerical error and incorrect physics. Pass on
parameters, original measurements, dynamics functions and accuracy limitations. When designing
the controller next month, account for model validity limits and actuator saturation; calling a
simulation function alone is insufficient.

Assigned reading: a focused reading route

Books and lecture notes may be in English; keep the plan, definitions and your own explanations in

Key terms and definitions

Rigid body. An idealisation in which the distances between any two points of a body remain
constant during motion and loading.
Centre of mass. The point whose position is the average position of all body elements, weighted
by their masses.
Inertia tensor. A matrix describing mass distribution about a selected point; it determines the
rotational contribution to kinetic energy and has units kg·m².
Free-body diagram. A diagram of an isolated body showing all external forces and torques,
including reactions from removed constraints.
Generalised coordinates. Independent configuration parameters sufficient to describe the
system’s position under its geometric constraints.
Lagrangian. The difference between kinetic and potential energy, used in Lagrange’s equations to
obtain system dynamics.
Mass matrix. The configuration-dependent matrix M(q), which determines kinetic energy and
relates generalised accelerations to forces.
Velocity-dependent terms. The part of the dynamics arising from coordinate motion, including
Coriolis and centrifugal effects; the representation of matrix C is not unique.
Gravity vector. Generalised torques or forces corresponding to the potential-energy gradient
under the chosen equation-of-motion convention.
Forward dynamics. Calculation of accelerations from current coordinates, velocities, applied
forces and the chosen constraint model.
Inverse dynamics. Calculation of required generalised forces from specified coordinates,
velocities and accelerations with known external inputs.
Dissipation. Irreversible conversion of mechanical energy into other forms, such as heat, through
friction or damping.
Unilateral contact. A constraint that permits bodies to separate and contact to be compressed,
but does not permit an attractive normal force between surfaces.
Normal force. The component of contact force along the surface normal; in a model without
adhesion it points away from the surface.
Friction cone. The set of admissible contact forces in the Coulomb model: tangential force
magnitude is bounded by μ times the normal force.
Sticking. A contact mode with zero relative tangential velocity, possible while the required force
remains within the friction limit.
Impulse. The time integral of force, which changes momentum; it approximates a short impact
without detailed calculation of peak force.
Support polygon. The convex hull of support points on a plane; the centre-of-mass projection
provides only a quasi-static criterion.
Check the relationships: M(q)q̈+c(q,q̇)+g(q)=τ+Jᵀf. All forces must have consistent signs, bases
and points of application.

Motion fundamentals: why torque is needed

Required: derive the equation of motion in two ways
The kinematics in M8 relates angles to endpoint position. Dynamics determines which torque
produces a chosen acceleration and what happens with a limited actuator. Before deriving the
equations, clarify the distinction between mass m in kg, moment of inertia I in kg·m² and torque τ
in N·m. A geometrically admissible leg or finger trajectory may demand unavailable torque.

One joint: geometry, energy and equation
Example pendulum: a point mass m=0.5 kg at the end of a massless rod l=0.2 m; the pivot is fixed.
In the x-y plane, y points upward and q is measured from the downward vertical towards +x. Then
p=(l sinq;-l cosq), v=q̇ in rad/s, I=ml²=0.02 kg·m². There is no friction or contact; positive τ_act
increases q.
Newton and Euler: the torque of weight about the pivot is -mgl sinq, so Iq̈=τ_act-mgl sinq.
Lagrange: K=Iv²/2, U=mgl(1-cosq), L=K-U. Substitution into d/dt(∂L/∂v)-∂L/∂q=τ_act gives the same
equation, Iq̈+mgl sinq=τ_act. These are two descriptions of the same mechanics; their agreement
helps check the signs.
At q=π/6, an acceleration q̈=1 rad/s² requires, by inverse dynamics,
τ_act=0.02·1+0.5·9.81·0.2·0.5=0.5105 N·m. Of this, 0.4905 N·m compensates for weight and
0.0200 N·m accelerates the mass. Forward dynamics calculates q̈=(τ_act-mgl sinq)/I. With τ_act=0
at this position, q̈=-24.525 rad/s²: the link accelerates towards the downward vertical, even when
starting from rest.

From a pendulum to two links
For a fixed-base chain of revolute joints: M(q)v̇+h(q,v)+g(q)=τ_act+Jᵀf_ext. Here v=q̇; M relates
accelerations to torques, h contains velocity effects, and g contains gravitational torques; f_ext is
the force exerted by the environment on the endpoint, expressed in the same basis as the
positional Jacobian J. Geometry determines centre-of-mass velocities, from which energy and M are
calculated. At rest, τ_act=g-Jᵀf_ext; motion adds inertial and velocity-dependent terms.
An actuator with a position interface does not become a source of known torque: use the validated
M7 model. A moving body, distributed contacts and spatial tensors require a more detailed model;
this module requires a clear pendulum calculation and the specified 2R dynamics.

Check torque, acceleration and energy calculations
In tasks 3-6, save both derivations and the numerical example. Independently estimate dU/dq
using a central difference: at q=π/6, expect 0.4905 N·m. Apply 0.5105 N·m to forward dynamics
and recover 1 rad/s². Then, without actuation or friction, compare energy E=K+U at two integrator
step sizes; reducing the step should reduce numerical drift. Agreement between inverse and
forward dynamics does not rule out an error shared by both functions.
Included in the 75 hours: 1 hour from 9-10 for the two derivations; 1 hour from 15-16 for the
pendulum; 1 hour from 33-34 for the forward and inverse dynamics check. This is a required
reference example within the existing tasks. Calculations run on the CPU; no additional equipment,
software or hours are required.
References: Tedrake, The Simple Pendulum: equation of motion and the Undamped Pendulum
section; Lynch/Park, Modern Robotics, §8.1.1 and §8.1.4: Lagrange, Newton and Euler methods.

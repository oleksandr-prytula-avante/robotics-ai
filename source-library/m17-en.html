Monthly outcome: a manipulator under
human supervision

M17 • 15 Feb 2028 - 19 Mar 2028 • 75 hours

Build a small, reproducible testbed for grasping and transferring an object. Treat it as an integrated
robotic system connecting kinematics, planning, operator actions, data, commands, updates and
engineering responsibility. The M17 deliverables are a simulated transfer of a simple object, a
study of repeatability and failures, an interface for selecting modes and cancelling actions, and a
documented assessment of threats and hazards.

Four areas of work
This month covers manipulation, human-robot interaction, ethics and cybersecurity, industrial
applications and teamwork. Allocate the manipulator work so that every topic receives attention. A
complex anthropomorphic hand, tactile skin and force-controlled grasping are not required here.
Under the curriculum, the advanced OPT block on hand mechanics and tactile sensors takes 120
hours after M17 and is separate from these 75 hours.

What to bring from M14-M16
From M14, use the object-observation format and the descriptions of coordinate frames and
uncertainty; from M16, use the prepared manipulator scene, collision checks, pre-grasp pose and
trajectory validation. In the main experiment, specify the object's position precisely in simulation to
distinguish manipulation errors from vision errors. Then deliberately offset the estimated position
and orientation. A complete vision-guided grasping system is outside this month's required project.

Testbed and working environment
Prepare an existing manipulator model with a simple parallel gripper, a table, a cube or cylinder,
and a target region. Work in Ubuntu 24.04 with ROS 2 Jazzy and a compatible MoveIt 2
configuration. You may additionally test your own mechanism once the baseline simulation
experiment works; rated limits and real contacts require separate tests. A cancel button in a
graphical interface does not, by itself, constitute a hardware emergency stop.
By the end of the month, explain why the manipulator might fail to reach its goal, who is authorised
to issue commands and how the operator knows the current mode. State which data are retained,
what happens when communication is lost and what must be checked before changing a limit. For
the future hexapod, the same questions concern body motion, leg contacts and handing control to
a human.

75 hours and a separate hand project

Four blocks
1-20: FK and IK, grasp coordinate frames, object transfer, contact and repeatability; 10 hours of
theory and analysis +10 of practice. 21-40: teleoperation, levels of autonomy, modes, operator
feedback and a small interface study; 10+10. 41-60: privacy, data errors, threat modelling,
authentication, encryption and updates; 10+10. 61-75: PLCs, industrial buses, standards,
requirements, verification and planning a team exercise; 5+10. Total: 35 hours of analysis and 40
of practice.
In the first three blocks, schedule reading in 2-hour sessions; in the last block, use 2+2+1. Divide
practical work into four Sunday blocks of 10 hours, or equal periods that suit your schedule. Page
dates follow the original curriculum, while step numbers indicate the learning workload.
Overlapping module boundaries, rest, holidays and separate checkpoints do not add hours; OPT120
is counted separately.

Keeping the scope manageable
Start with one robot, one object type and one scene, then introduce variations. Keep development
of custom motor control, vision and a physical hand outside this experiment. MoveIt plans the
geometric sequence of actions; assess contact and object retention using separate simulator
observations. A simplified object attachment demonstrates planning, but does not establish that
grasping force is sufficient.

Preparation in the first block
Create a model-description file: simulator and MoveIt versions, object dimensions, joint limits, table
position, coordinate frames and the source of the pose estimate. For each experiment, save run_id,
the random-number generator seed, parameters, initial object state and result. Provide observation
without issuing commands, manual control, plan execution and stopping. Maintain a shared log of
command_id, mode and actor, even while there is only one operator.
Working folders: manipulation/ for tasks and scenes; operator/ for the interface and study protocol;
evaluation/ for repeated experiments; review/ for hazards, threats and ethical decisions;
requirements/ for criteria and changes. Store secret keys and participants' personal data outside
the repository. In the report, distinguish observations, proposed causes and ways to test them: this
helps another engineering team understand the system.

Manipulation: poses, approach and contact

What to study
Review forward kinematics, FK: it determines end-effector position and orientation from joint
coordinates. Inverse kinematics, IK, solves the reverse problem; there may be several valid
solutions or none. Define the world, base, tool, object and grasp coordinate frames. Define the
grasp pose relative to the object and the pre-grasp pose as an offset along the approach direction.
Before connecting the planner, verify transform composition and inversion using three known
poses.
Transfer sequence: move into position → approach → close the gripper → confirm retention → lift →
transfer → lower → open → withdraw. Define geometric and logical conditions for each stage. The
attach operation in the planning scene changes the collision model, but does not measure contact.
When introduced to force and impedance control, establish how position control differs from
specifying a desired interaction; a complete force-control loop is studied separately.

Task 1. FK, IK and the grasp coordinate frame
Use an existing manipulator model and three reachable object poses. Specify the grasp offset,
calculate the tool pose and solve IK while checking joint limits and self-collisions. Calculate FK for
the solution and compare its position and orientation with the target. Save a table of poses,
solutions and rejection reasons. Test an object outside the workspace and a rotation of 180°; if no
valid IK solution exists, the limits must remain enabled.

Task 2. A complete grasp-and-transfer cycle
Use MoveIt Task Constructor or a compatible sequence of ROS actions. Save each stage and its
result, add the table and object to the scene, and check attached geometry during transfer.
Prepare a task description, configuration and reproducible demonstration. Separately verify that
the object reached the target region and was released, and that the arm withdrew: successfully
producing a plan without executing it must not count as task success.

Interpreting contact checks
For a physical simulation of retention, specify mass, friction and the gripper model; check whether
the object can fall. When using a simplified attach, label the mode a kinematic demonstration
(kinematic demo). Consider a virtual spring F=K·(x_des−x), with K in N/m, and a damper. Explain
why high stiffness and delay can increase contact forces. Specified forces and speeds are
educational parameters and do not replace checking rated specifications and conducting physical
tests.

Repeatability and operator interaction

Task 3. Defining a successful transfer
Perform 10 runs with the same initial scene and 10 with small, predefined object offsets. Save
success status and failure stage, final pose, cycle time and retention state. Before the experiments,
specify the target region in metres and acceptable orientation. Present every result, mean error
and dispersion; if deterministic simulation produces identical results, explain why.
Repeatability describes how close results are under identical conditions; accuracy describes
closeness to a specified reference. An incorrect grasp offset can make the arm place the object in
the same wrong location every time: test this case. In separate experiments, introduce an incorrect
mass, friction or calibration offset and distinguish these causes. In a kinematic demonstration,
evaluate the plan and pose; such an experiment does not measure holding force.

Human-robot interaction (HRI)
Study direct teleoperation, assisted control, autonomous task execution and explicit transfer of
control. The operator needs the current mode, active goal, age of state data, reason for rejection
and available action. A mode error occurs when a person expects manual control while an
autonomous plan is active, or the reverse. Spatial interaction requires clearly indicated access
zones and the robot's future path; low speed alone does not demonstrate contact safety.

Task 4. Modes and operator intervention
Create a simple panel or CLI with view, manual, auto and stopped states. Include command_id and
mode in commands. Before a manual↔auto transition, complete or cancel the previous ROS action;
after stopped, the old queue must not resume automatically. Save the transition table, interface
and test log. Test a manual request during auto and a repeated start after communication loss.
Give the operator a scenario: position the gripper, start a transfer and stop it when the object is
wrong. Display the planned path and current stage; present debugging diagnostics separately. The
same principle applies to transferring control of gait: a human action must bring the system into a
clear state, otherwise competing commands may enter a shared topic.

Interface testing and privacy

Task 5. Test the interface on a clearly defined task
Conduct a pilot study in simulation with two interface versions: for example, buttons alone and
buttons with explicit mode and state indications. Use the same task: start a transfer, notice an
intentionally incorrect goal and cancel the action. Explain the task and the data to be recorded to
the volunteer beforehand. Conduct this experiment without physical robot motion near a person. If
no participant is available, follow a predefined scenario yourself and state that no independent
study took place.
Prepare 3-5 attempts for each version, alternate their order and provide the same initial training.
Record the time until correct cancellation, mode errors, unnecessary actions and a brief answer to
“what is the robot doing now?”. Save the protocol, anonymised table and three observations. So
few attempts cannot establish statistical superiority; use them to correct specific unclear
indications.

Task 6. Camera data and model errors
List the testbed's data: table images, object pose, operator actions, identifiers and logs. For each
type, state its purpose and retention period, access rights and deletion method. In the
demonstration, retain only the necessary table region or use synthetic data. If people or personal
belongings appear in the frame, exclude unnecessary regions from the educational dataset in
advance; transfer to an external service requires an appropriate basis.
Return to the baseline CNN from M14 and compare errors under two lighting conditions and with
two object materials. Consider practical ethical questions: for whom and under which conditions
does the system perform worse, who knows its limitations and who can correct the result? If the
data contain no people, evaluate gaps in operating conditions and interface accessibility; there is
no basis here for demographic statistics. Good average performance does not establish
performance under unstudied conditions.

A documented decision
Write a short record: “question → alternatives → chosen decision → verification → remaining
limitation”. For example, events and anonymised errors suffice to measure cancellation time, so
operator video need not be retained. If pose-estimation confidence is low, request another
observation before grasping. Include these decisions and supporting results in the final engineering
review.

Command security: a threat model for your
testbed

Distinguish hazards from threats
A hazard is a potential source of harm, such as unexpected gripper motion. A cybersecurity threat
is the possibility of malicious interference, such as an unauthorised command or a substituted
update. A code error, malfunction or attack may lead to the same consequence. For each case,
record the cause, protection and test. Encryption does not correct an invalid joint limit, and a limit
switch does not determine access rights to video recordings.

Task 7. A threat model for your own testbed
Draw the testbed boundaries: operator, ROS and DDS network, control process, storage and update
channel. For each flow, identify the protected asset, sender, recipient, trust boundary and possible
violation. Analyse unauthorised commands, replay of old commands, calibration changes,
corrupted updates and log leaks. Prepare a table of 5-8 specific threats with priorities and owners
of protective measures.
In an isolated experiment, test commands containing an unknown actor, expired timestamp,
repeated command_id, NaN and out-of-range values. The expected result is rejection with an
explanation and no simulated motion. DDS certificates, identity and permissions govern admission
to the system and its channels; the application checks command freshness and prevents repeated
execution. Enabling SROS2 alone does not protect against all these threats.

Task 8. Minimum protection and an update
Study authentication, authorisation, encryption and trust in updates. On the local testbed,
configure minimum permissions for one publisher and subscriber using SROS2, or reproduce the
official example in a separate namespace. Test permitted and prohibited publication. Save a public
description of roles and permissions and verification logs; exclude private keys from the final
package.
Prepare an educational configuration-update scenario. A new version of the limits requires a
contents description, a hash and engineering review; a deliberately modified file must be rejected,
and the previous working version must be restored in simulation. Explain that a hash detects
change, but verifying authorship requires a trusted signature or channel. Parameters may change
during motion only through a defined state transition.

Industrial applications: the cell and its
requirements

What to understand at an introductory level
A PLC executes industrial cycle logic and processes inputs and outputs; the robot controller
controls drives and the programme; a supervisory computer may plan actions and exchange data.
An industrial bus connects devices and defines communication properties. Learn the purposes of
EtherCAT, PROFINET and CAN; implementing these protocols is outside this month's scope.
Distinguish ordinary control from separate protective functions.
ISO 10218 concerns industrial robots and their applications; ISO 13849 addresses safety-related
parts of control systems; IEC 61508 provides the context of functional safety and its lifecycle. In
M17, read official descriptions of scope and terminology, without claiming compliance with a
standard. Applicability, the required level and verification of a real cell require separate input data
and competent assessment.

Task 9. Sketch an educational cell
Draw the robot, table, object feed, presence sensor, operator panel and a notional PLC. Define a
ready/request/busy/done/fault state exchange with cycle_id and test it using software process
simulators. Save the diagram, I/O table and event sequence. Test a repeated request, a missing
part, communication loss and reset during busy. The done state belongs to a particular cycle_id: an
old event must not confirm processing of a new part.

Task 10. Requirements, changes and teamwork
Formulate 8-10 requirements: start conditions, acceptable scene, cancel behaviour, pose-estimate
age, operator rights, logging and rejection on recovery. For each, provide a criterion and a link to
its test. Change the grasp offset or speed and prepare a change request: reason, affected
requirements, checks and restoration of the previous version. Support engineering review with
data; label your own verification as self-review.
Plan how to gain teamwork experience: which part of the work can be submitted for review, which
questions to ask a control or mechanical specialist, and which small contribution to a robotics
project would demonstrate collaboration. Prepare a review package and a list of missing
experience, such as industrial equipment, initial hardware commissioning and working with
someone else's requirements. An educational testbed does not replace an internship or a verified
professional qualification.

Eight checks for manipulation, the operator
and threats

Manipulator and interface
S1 - unreachable pose: IK or the planner reports failure, and the system does not enter executing.
S2 - the object touches the table during transfer: the collision model includes the attached object
and rejects the plan. S3 - incorrect grasp offset: the robot misses repeatedly, and the report
distinguishes repeatability from accuracy. In a physical simulation, add slipping; in a kinematic
demonstration, attach does not confirm retention.
S4 - mode change during auto: a manual request triggers cancel, waits for coordinated completion
and only then allows a manual command. Mode outputs are not mixed. S5 - loss of communication
or pose estimates: data age increases, and the task receives the defined stopped or uncertain
status. Network recovery does not start the old queue. Describe the simulator's actual response;
this experiment does not demonstrate a certified protective stop.

Commands, data and the cycle
S6 - repeated command or unknown actor: the application rejects the request without repeating
the cycle. Check command_id, mode and validity period separately from DDS participant
identification. S7 - configuration change: integrity checking detects substitution, the update is not
activated and the previous version can be reproduced. Separately state that without a trusted
signature, forged authorship remains possible.
S8 - late done from the previous PLC cycle: the new request has a different cycle_id, so the old
event does not count as success. Add an absent object after a claimed grasp: the hold-check stage
must report the corresponding failure. Inspect the final archive manually: it must contain only
necessary frames and events, without participants' personal data or secret keys.

Recording criteria
For each scenario, record the initial scene, triggering event, expected states, observed response,
timing and remaining risk. Justify pose and timing tolerances as parameters of the educational
task. This table does not define safe speeds for physical interaction with a person. The absence of
errors in 10 repetitions does not prove zero failure probability.
Indicate how each result is supported: simulation, logic or document review, or a required physical
test. Show the operator the reason for failure, whether motion has been cancelled and whether
restarting is possible. If the execution outcome is still unknown, state that explicitly: hidden
uncertainty prevents an accurate assessment of robot state.

Acceptance checks and preparation for the
hand project

Evidence for the four areas
Manipulation: scene, configuration, complete cycle or explicitly labelled kinematic demonstration,
FK and IK verification, and a table of repeated trials. HRI: modes, cancellation, indications and a
protocol for the participant study or self-conducted walkthrough. Ethics and cybersecurity: data
inventory, use decisions, threat model and results of isolated tests. Industrial applications: cell
diagram, I/O, requirements, change request and a plan to develop teamwork experience.
Prepare a README and a unified set of commands to reproduce the work. State in the report what
was measured physically, what was tested in simulation and what was considered only
theoretically. These experiments do not establish ISO/IEC certification, safe collaborative operation
or industrial readiness. Save observed outcomes for the eight scenarios, and supporting results or
a description of the remaining limitation for every significant protective measure.

Self-check questions
1. How does the tool pose differ from the object pose? 2. Why can IK have several valid solutions?
3. How does an attached object affect collision checking? 4. Why does attach not measure grasping
force? 5. How does repeatability differ from accuracy? 6. How does the operator know who
currently controls the robot?
7. What happens after cancel and communication loss? 8. How does a hazard differ from a
cybersecurity threat? 9. Why does encryption not replace limit checks? 10. What does a hash prove
about an update, and what does it not prove? 11. How does cycle_id prevent a false done? 12.
What external experience do you still need to work in a robotics team?

What to hand over to OPT120
Hand over coordinate-frame descriptions, the baseline arm model, scene with objects, grasp
parameters, reproducible action sequence, operator-intervention requirements and failure log.
With this foundation, the separate 120-hour block after M17 develops hand mechanics, tactile
perception, retention and physical testing. Its hours, equipment and criteria are accounted for
separately; a completed anthropomorphic hand is not an M17 deliverable.
For the walking robot, retain the rules for mode selection, command authority, command-freshness
checks and review of limits. They will be needed for external control of the body and legs. Before
use on a real robot, test these decisions with the relevant mechanics, loads and actuator
electronics. M17 is complete when you can explain and reproduce the entire testbed's operation,
including failure and recovery.

Assigned reading: manipulation and
human-robot interaction

Read the specified extracts before the corresponding tasks. Core reading explains principles;
reference reading supports implementation. Sources may be in English; prepare your explanations
and report in English. Reading is included in the 75 hours.

Core reading
1. Russ Tedrake, Robotic Manipulation (MIT, course notes). Basic Pick and Place: Monogram
Notation; Pick and place via spatial transforms. For Tasks 1-2: write down the object → grasp → tool
transforms and one pre-grasp pose; other chapters are not needed now.
2. Christoph Bartneck et al., Human-Robot Interaction: An Introduction, 2nd ed., 2024.
Chapters Design, Research Methods, Robots in Society. For Tasks 4-6: one mode-indication
principle, a pilot-study protocol and a data decision; skim Spatial Interaction to define the
boundaries of the motion zone.
3. Ross Anderson, Security Engineering, 3rd ed. Ch. 4 Protocols; Ch. 6 Access Control. For
Tasks 7-8: selected examples of authentication, message replay and permissions. After reading,
distinguish an unauthorised command, an old command and an incorrect but permitted parameter.

Mechanics reference reading
4. Kevin Lynch, Frank Park, Modern Robotics. §12.1.2 Contact Types; §12.2.1 Friction; §11.5
Force Control. For Task 3 and the transition to OPT120: distinguish touching, slipping and holding,
and position and force. Leave the full theory of force-based grasping to the separate block.

Laboratory documentation
MoveIt Task Constructor: Pick and Place - stages and scene for Task 2; check main documentation
against your pinned Jazzy version. SROS2: README and tutorials on the Jazzy branch - participant
identification and permissions in Task 8, with permitted and prohibited communication.
Official ISO/IEC scope descriptions for Tasks 9-10 are listed on the preparation page. After reading,
prepare a cell diagram, one verifiable requirement and applicability limits. Reading the standards
in full is not required this month.

Fundamental concepts in M17

Pose. A body's position and orientation relative to a specified coordinate frame; both components
are needed to describe a grasp.
Grasp frame. A coordinate frame on the object relative to which the desired end-effector position
and orientation are specified.
Inverse kinematics (IK). Finding joint coordinates for a specified pose; a valid solution must
respect limits and be checked using forward kinematics.
Pre-grasp pose (pregrasp). Position and orientation before approaching the object; it defines
the approach direction and space for the open gripper.
Attached object. Geometry linked to the robot in the planning scene; this link alone does not
establish physical retention of the object.
Repeatability. Closeness of repeated transfer results under identical conditions; a systematic
offset may persist in every attempt.
Accuracy. Closeness of a result to a specified reference; it is evaluated relative to a defined goal,
coordinate frame and measurement conditions.
Impedance. A specified dynamic relationship between motion and interaction force; it usually
includes virtual stiffness and damping.
HRI. The study and design of human-robot interaction, including state understanding, control,
errors and social context.
Mode error. An operator action based on an incorrect understanding of the current mode or of
who controls the robot.
Hazard. A potential source of harm; the presence of a hazard alone does not describe an event's
probability or the severity of its consequences.
Security threat. A possible violation of protected system properties, such as command
substitution, data disclosure or loss of control availability.
Authentication. Verification of a participant's claimed identity or identifier by a trusted method; it
does not itself define permitted actions.
Authorisation. A decision to permit a particular action by an identified participant, considering
role, resource and access policy.
Encryption. Transforming data to conceal their content from those without the key; it does not
replace checks of command authority, freshness and admissibility.
Hash. A short computed representation of content; comparison detects change relative to a
trusted reference, but does not establish authorship.
Digital signature. Cryptographic verification of integrity and of a message's association with the
signer's key, assuming trust in the corresponding public key.
Idempotency. A processing property whereby repeating the same logical command does not
repeat the physical action or change.
PLC. A programmable logic controller that executes industrial cycle logic and processes inputs and
outputs according to its configuration.
Cycle handshake. An exchange of readiness, request and completion states under defined rules;
cycle_id links acknowledgement to a particular operation.

Motion fundamentals: fingers and grasping

Required: touching is not yet retention
Kinematics establishes whether contact has been reached; statics establishes whether admissible
forces can balance the object's weight and moments. First draw the object and finger separately,
showing pairs of equal and opposite forces and the moment arm. For the finger, f_ext always
denotes the force exerted by the object on the robot; the robot's force on the object points in the
opposite direction.

A simple retention calculation
Consider a stationary object between two symmetric vertical fingers. Each contact has a normal
force N and upward friction no greater than μN; the weight mg acts downward. For a centred object
without an external rotational moment, 2μN≥mg is necessary. This checks that particular vertical
equilibrium; it does not prove retention against every possible disturbance.
With m=0.20 kg, g=9.81 m/s² and μ=0.50: mg=1.962 N and N≥1.962 N per finger. Choosing
N=2.5 N gives a maximum total friction force of 2.5 N. If μ falls to 0.25, the limit becomes 1.25 N
and can no longer support the weight. Equality at the boundary provides no margin; μ here is an
illustrative model parameter.
For a massless vertical link with a normal-force moment arm of 0.04 m, the holding torque
magnitude is 0.04·1.962≈0.0785 N·m; friction in this geometry acts along the link. A real finger
requires its Jacobian, mass and configuration: in statics, τ_act+τ_g+Jᵀf_ext=0, where τ_g denotes
joint torques due to gravity. Changing the external force direction also changes the sign of the
compensating torque.

Why one motor does not determine two angles
For a finger with two independent joints, one taut, inextensible tendon can impose only
Δl=r1q1+r2q2 for constant moment arms. With r1=r2=0.01 m and Δl=0.005 m, q1+q2=0.5 rad:
the pairs (0.1;0.4) and (0.3;0.2) both satisfy the command. The actual pose depends on springs,
contact and dynamics. This illustrates underactuation; when an object stops one link, the
distribution of motion in the other changes.

Two exercises for Tasks 2-3
1. In the object-transfer report, save a force diagram and a table for three values of μ and two
masses. With N=2.5 N, predict by hand whether the object will be held or will slip. Compare with
contact simulation if available; with simplified attach, label the result a model calculation. Planner
success does not establish this equilibrium.
2. Plot the line q1+q2=0.5 and add the contact condition q1=0.2 rad: this leaves q2=0.3 rad. Save
the plot and explain which physical condition determined the configuration. A full-size hand will
need separate mechanics and sensors; a calculation without purchases suffices for now. Do not
send torque commands to position-controlled servos.
Time allocation: 1 hour from 7-8 for statics; 1 hour from 17-18 to check the table as part of Task
3, and 1 hour from 19-20 to explain failure. These are within the original 75 hours. Building the
hand and working with tactile sensors belong to the separate OPT120 stage after M17.
Assigned reading: Lynch/Park, Modern Robotics §5.2 and §12.2.1 - torque and friction; Tedrake,
Fully-actuated vs Underactuated Systems: Definitions - independent coordinates and available
control inputs.

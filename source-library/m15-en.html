Monthly outcome: where the robot is

M15 • 19 Dec 2027 - 20 Jan 2028 • 75 hours

Compare motion-based position estimation, localisation on a known map and joint reconstruction of
a map and trajectory. Deliver a reproducible setup for 2D and visual SLAM, a trajectory-error table
and an analysis of conditions that make estimates unreliable. This introduces global navigation for
the future walking robot; a complete global-navigation implementation does not become a
prerequisite for stable walking.

Connecting the body, legs and camera
The robot moves its body, measures motion with an IMU, observes surfaces with a camera and
sometimes knows its support positions. Wheel odometry uses wheel geometry; leg odometry uses
kinematics and an assumption of stationary contact; visual odometry uses an image sequence;
inertial odometry integrates angular velocity and acceleration. For each source, record an
assumption that can fail: slip, loss of contact, lack of texture or sensor bias.

What to bring from earlier modules
You need transformation matrices, a basic EKF, ROS 2 tf/rosbag, RGB-D loading and the M14
understanding of ICP. Check the yaw sign, quaternion component order, time units and
camera-to-body transform. If these checks fail, first correct them using three known positions and
orientations. Do not try to compensate for a coordinate-frame error by tuning filter noise.

Minimum experimental setup
The main 2D experiment uses a short simulator recording containing LaserScan, odometry, IMU
and exact ground truth. Use one route: a straight section, a turn, a return to the start and a
repetitive corridor. For the visual method, use an RGB-D sequence from the same simulator or a
TUM recording with a reference trajectory. If the scenes differ, compare failure causes and
normalised measures. Incomparable ATE values do not establish that one sensor is more accurate
than another.
Continue in Ubuntu 24.04 and ROS 2 Jazzy. Use compatible versions of slam_toolbox and the
chosen visual method; save versions and parameters. You do not need to write a second complete
SLAM system from scratch: integrate an existing baseline or build a small pose graph from RGB-D
odometry and verified loop closures. Save a map, trajectory and quantitative evaluation; an RViz
visualisation alone is insufficient.

Organising 75 hours and comparing methods

Time allocation
1-20: odometry, coordinate frames, EKF and error growth; 10 hours of analysis and 10 of practice.
21-40: occupancy grids, inverse sensor models, scan matching and AMCL; 10+10. 41-60: pose
graphs, nonlinear least squares, covariance and loop closure; 10+10. 61-75: ATE/RPE, degraded
data, LiDAR-versus-vision comparison and acceptance; 5+10. Total: 35 hours of theory and
analysis and 40 of practice.
Each 10-hour theory block comprises five 2-hour sessions; split the final 5 hours as 2+2+1. Place
four 10-hour practicals in Sunday work intervals or divide them without increasing the total time.
The numbers below denote module study hours. Overlapping module-boundary dates do not
double the available time; retain holidays, rest and separate integration checkpoints from the main
roadmap.

Fix the protocol before running algorithms
Save the sequence duration and boundaries, input sensors, frequencies, exact reference-trajectory
source and map resolution. State whether the algorithm knows the initial position and orientation.
Ground truth is only for error calculation; SLAM must not receive it through an accessible
coordinate frame. Accidentally supplying ideal odometry invalidates the experiment.
Create bags/manifest, configs/lidar, configs/visual, trajectories, maps, evaluation and failures
directories. For each run, save run_id, random-number seed, commit, noise parameters, data and
trajectory-alignment method. Include matched timestamps, excluded intervals and the
tracked-route fraction in evaluation, alongside aggregate metrics. Otherwise, a method that loses
half the route can appear more accurate than a reliable baseline.

Experiment without searching every combination
Change one parameter class at a time: odometry noise, scan dropouts, initial offset or loop-closure
threshold. Use three interpretable levels and retain one route. Tune on a separate sequence and
evaluate the final result on a held-out one. You do not need to study all of Nav2: use only
localisation and the map in M15. First verify visual SLAM on a short valid recording, then on one
degraded-data episode; leave analysis time after installation.

Odometry and EKF: estimating motion with
uncertainty

Essential concepts
Dead reckoning accumulates small displacements: for a planar body, x_new = x + v·cos(yaw)·dt,
y_new = y + v·sin(yaw)·dt, yaw_new = yaw + w·dt. Here v is in m/s, w in rad/s and dt in seconds.
This discretisation suits a teaching calculation with small steps; for rapid turns, check the midpoint
method or exact model integration. Bias is a constant offset, whereas noise is a varying deviation;
they affect the trajectory differently.
In odom, the local trajectory should remain continuous even as drift accumulates. The map frame
allows global map-based correction. Study the map → odom → base_link chain and the sensor's
fixed pose relative to the body. One agreed source must publish each transform. A jump in the
global estimate must not silently become a jump in the local controller's command.

Task 1. Body drift on a closed route
Generate a square trajectory with stops and saved true positions and orientations. Separately add
velocity noise, a constant yaw-rate bias and slip on one turn. Integrate odometry and plot error
against time and distance. Output: three trajectories and a parameter log. Test zero noise, dt=0
and timestamp discontinuity; no motion must mean no progress along the route.

Task 2. Fuse measurements and check covariance
Use state [x,y,yaw], a motion model and periodic absolute position measurements in simulation.
Write out prediction and correction steps, residual and covariance. Implement a compact EKF or
adapt a verified example from earlier modules; compare with configured robot_localization on ROS
data. Do not count the same information twice: position and velocity derived from the same
encoder are correlated.
Output: the estimate, uncertainty ellipses and an innovation plot. Test missing absolute
measurements, an anomalous jump and an artificially small R. For the leg, separately explain how
an incorrect stationary-foot assumption produces false confidence. The task criterion is explainable
error growth and filter responses, rather than a promise that EKF always improves the estimate. A
poor model or wrong units can make it worse than simple odometry.

Maps and scan matching: what
measurements provide

This week's topics
An occupancy grid stores the probability that a cell is occupied, not floor height. The inverse sensor
model uses a ray: cells before a reliable return were observed free, cells at the return are probably
occupied, and cells behind it remain unknown. Study log-odds L = log(p/(1−p)), which is convenient
for updates. Limit confidence accumulated from old observations. Define separate rules for missing
measurements and maximum range.
2D scan matching estimates a transform between scans or between a scan and a map. ICP
depends on correspondences and its initial estimate; a uniform long corridor poorly constrains
motion along its length. AMCL represents a distribution of possible positions and orientations as
particles on a known map: it localises rather than constructs a map. Compare a broad initial
distribution across the map with a precisely specified initial pose.

Task 3. Build a map from known poses
Input: 200-500 simulated LaserScan messages and true poses, available only to this reference task.
Transform laser points into the world, update free cells along rays and occupied cells at endpoints.
Output: a grid, an occupied/free/unknown legend and the resolution setting. Check one ray and a
wall by hand; do not mark the unseen side of a wall as free. Repeat with twice the cell size and
compare wall thickness.

Task 4. LiDAR recording and localisation
Run slam_toolbox on the same route with ordinary odometry; save the pose graph and map. Then
stop mapping and localise on the fixed map using AMCL or a supported localization mode. Do not
run two map→odom publishers simultaneously. Save launch and config files, two trajectories, the
map and a list of TF checks. Compare with your reference map from Task 3.
Test an incorrect initial pose, loss of 20% of scans and a laser rotation in the extrinsics. For scan
matching, create a small pair with a known displacement and measure transform error, then use an
ambiguous corridor pair. Separate mapping error from localisation error on an already known map
in the report. Successful loop closure does not establish correct local geometry along every section.

Pose graphs: constraints, loop closure and
false links

Sections to study
A graph vertex represents the robot's position and orientation, or pose; an edge represents
measured relative motion or another constraint. Optimisation adjusts poses to reduce weighted
residuals. Weights relate to uncertainty: using identical numbers for metres and radians without
justification creates an arbitrary trade-off. Fixing one initial pose removes ambiguity in overall
translation and rotation, but adds no measurements of the surroundings.
Loop closure recognises a previously visited place and adds a constraint. The optimiser reconciles
constraints but does not verify correspondence truth. A robust loss reduces the influence of large
residuals; it does not replace geometric verification and may fail to protect against a consistent
false closure. Treat covariance as a local estimate within model assumptions, rather than the
probability that the whole map is correct.

Task 5. A small graph you can understand
Create 20-30 planar poses around a square, noisy odometry edges and one correct loop-closure
edge. Use GTSAM Pose2 or a small educational solver. Show initial and optimised trajectories,
residuals and the method used to anchor the common coordinate frame. Save graph.json, noise
settings and plots before and after optimisation. Test zero noise and a disabled prior; explain why
absolute coordinates are undetermined without an anchor.

Task 6. False loop closure in a similar corridor
Add an edge between different but visually similar places. Compare ordinary squared error, a
robust loss and geometric rejection of the incorrect link before optimisation. Measure ATE at the
same poses and changes in residuals of correct edges. Save three results and the loop-acceptance
rule. Show a case where the robust loss still allows map deformation; do not promise a universal
threshold.

A minimal visual-odometry experiment
Bring calibration, RGB-D recordings and pair-registration checks from M14; in M15, assemble a
short RGB-D odometry sequence. Build a graph of successive poses and add one revisit confirmed
by image or cloud geometry. Use ground truth only for error evaluation. You may use an existing
compatible visual SLAM system. If a loop closure is specified manually to test the optimiser, label it
as a manual constraint: that experiment does not test automatic revisit recognition.

Evaluation: trajectory, map and tracking loss

What ATE and RPE measure
ATE compares estimated absolute poses with a reference after explicitly described alignment. For
translation, report the RMSE of distances in metres in the usual formulation. RPE compares relative
displacements between pairs of poses separated by a fixed interval, revealing local drift. Specify
the interval in seconds, frames or metres and the timestamp-matching rule. Different intervals
answer different questions and produce incomparable values.
For metric RGB-D and LiDAR, do not rescale trajectories during alignment except in a separate
scale-error study. For a monocular baseline, state whether Sim(3) was used and which errors scale
fitting conceals. Alongside aligned error, save the original trajectory, route coverage and the time
tracking was lost. Do not evaluate only a successful short fragment while omitting the lost return
leg.

Task 7. An evaluator you can trust
Create a reference trajectory, a copy with a known translation, a copy with slow drift and a
time-shifted sequence. Run evo and independently calculate several pairs. Save commands,
alignment settings, matched timestamps and an ATE/RPE table. After SE(3)/SE(2) alignment,
overall translation and rotation should disappear while local drift remains. Specify the maximum
timestamp mismatch and show the number of rejected poses.

Task 8. Map quality and robustness
In the simulated scene, compare reconstructed walls with known geometry: distances to reference
lines, passage width and occupied/free-cell fractions in a predefined region. Do not count unknown
as free. For each pipeline, report latency, tracking-recovery time and route coverage. Choose
tolerances from map resolution and the clearance required by the future body; this is a teaching
requirement, not a regulatory standard.
Offset sensor and odometry timestamps, then change the laser extrinsics; repeat with algorithm
parameters unchanged. Explain why a synchronisation error can resemble a scan-matching error.
Add a blurred section and loss of texture to the visual experiment. The overall conclusion must
identify operating conditions for each method rather than declare one algorithm best under every
condition.

Laboratory comparison of LiDAR and visual
SLAM

Inputs and outputs
Inputs: recording manifest, calibration/frames and configs for three variants - odometry only, LiDAR
SLAM and visual RGB-D SLAM. Outputs: trajectory with timestamp and pose fields, tracking status,
a map or aligned keyframes, a loop-closure log and metrics. A separate error-evaluation
programme receives the reference trajectory. For each variant, state available sensors, whether
the starting pose is known and the source of metric scale.

Task 9. One question set for three methods
1. Select a valid sequence and a held-out difficult route. 2. Check TF and time bases without
running SLAM. 3. Compute odometry alone. 4. Run the LiDAR variant and save the map and graph.
5. Run the visual method with fixed parameters. 6. Evaluate matching available intervals and full
coverage separately. 7. Repeat with one fault. 8. Assemble a report explaining the causes of
differences.
If the camera and laser were recorded in the same simulator, compare identical
reference-trajectory sections. If RGB-D comes from TUM, create two tables: metrics within each
dataset and a qualitative comparison of failures. Claims such as "LiDAR is N times more accurate"
are invalid for different routes. Tabulate comparison conditions so scene differences remain
explicit.

Task 10. Robot response to lost localisation
Create a component that receives pose with status, timestamp and uncertainty. For stale data or
lost tracking, it must output localization_unavailable and reject new global goals in simulation. Do
not restart the running local stability loop. Recovery requires a new valid estimate, reconciliation of
map/odom and an explicit return transition. Save an event log and a missing-data test.

Deliverable package
The README must describe recording processing and error evaluation; the manifest documents
data provenance; configs contains every parameter. Include ATE/RPE with definitions, map quality,
latency, route coverage and failure scenarios in the report. Add a TF-tree snapshot and example
input/output messages. For the future hexapod, state the applicability boundary: global localisation
helps follow a route but does not validate leg stability or contact reliability.

СтраницаPage 8 · Eight failure and sensitivity scenarios
ROBOTICS AND AI  /  M15  /  DETAILED STUDY PLAN

Eight failure and sensitivity scenarios

Faults to introduce
S1 - No absolute observations: disable EKF corrections and loop closures along part of the route.
Expect growing drift and increased uncertainty if the model accounts for it. Compare with zero
noise and check for false confidence despite nonzero error. S2 - Slip: add odometry error on one
turn. Save the fault time, local RPE and correction after observing the surroundings.
S3 - Corridor ambiguity: walls repeat and transverse structure is sparse. Measure errors along and
across the corridor separately; a small ICP residual does not yet establish correct displacement. S4
- False closure: connect similar places with an incorrect edge. Evaluate map deformation, ATE and
changes in correct-edge residuals with and without a robust loss.
S5 - Kidnapped robot: in simulation, instantaneously move the body elsewhere on the known map
without a corresponding odometry change. Test AMCL with local and broad initial distributions and
measure recovery time under the chosen criterion. Manual reinitialisation must not be called
automatic relocalisation. S6 - Data loss: remove scans or images for 1 and 3 seconds. The
consumer must detect estimate age and unavailability.
S7 - Time and extrinsics: shift sensor timestamps by one period, then separately rotate the sensor
by a known angle. Compare errors on straight sections and turns. S8 - Image degradation: blur, a
smooth wall or a covered camera. Measure the fraction of tracked images and test recovery; errors
on the surviving images alone are insufficient. Do not remove failed sections before preparing the
final table.

Choosing levels and conclusions
Fault levels are illustrative parameters chosen to suit the measurement frequency and scene scale;
they are not allowable limits for a physical robot. Change one cause per experiment and preserve
the seed and original bag. Evaluate sensitivity at three levels; state whether improvement repeats
or occurs only on one route.
Each scenario needs an expected-result file, actual trace, numerical criterion or manual-check rule,
and a decision: handled, detected without recovery, or undetected. The last outcome is acceptable
as an established limitation of this introductory SLAM work, but a consumer must not present an
unreliable pose estimate as current and accurate.

Sessions 1-40: motion, the filter and the map

Hours 1-10: theory in 2-hour sessions
1-2: wheel, leg, visual and inertial odometry models, with one failure-prone assumption each. 3-4:
planar-motion integration and bias; calculate a turn and return by hand. 5-6: map/odom/base_link,
transform directions and TF sources. 7-8: EKF prediction and correction, Q/R and measurement
correlation. 9-10: ground truth, error units and the Task 1 protocol. Record these principles as
implementation assumptions.

Hours 11-20: practical 1
11-12: square-route generator and an error-free check. 13-14: Task 1 - noise, bias and local slip.
15-16: Task 2 - EKF, covariance and innovation plots. 17-18: process a ROS recording, compare
with the chosen filter and check TF. 19-20: missing measurements, excessively small R and an
error report. Understand the source of drift before connecting SLAM.

Hours 21-30: theory in 2-hour sessions
21-22: occupancy, free/occupied/unknown and log-odds. 23-24: inverse laser-ray model, resolution
and invalid range. 25-26: scan matching, ICP, local minima and the corridor. 27-28: particles,
measurement likelihood and AMCL resampling through an understandable example. 29-30:
slam_toolbox modes, the difference between a map and a graph, and state saving. Select the
manual parameters needed for your scenario.

Hours 31-40: practical 2
31-32: Task 3, a map from known poses and a manual ray check. 33-34: a lower-resolution map
and the unknown/free distinction. 35-36: Task 4, process the LiDAR SLAM recording and save the
graph and map. 37-38: localisation on a fixed map, initial offset and TF transform sources. 39-40:
corridor, scan loss and a comparison report against the reference map.
By this stage, there should be three clearly understood files: a reference map for evaluation, a
reconstructed map and pose estimates. Identify their roles in filenames and the manifest. If map
processing continues after playback stops, allow the queue to finish within the practical and record
processing lag; do not compare one method's partial result with another's completed result.

If time is limited
First reduce the number of long recordings and parameters, then omit extra optimisers and
detailed 3D visualisation. Retain processing of one LiDAR recording, one visual baseline, a small

Completion criteria and data for the planner

Acceptance checks
Quantitatively compare at least uncorrected odometry and SLAM on one sequence. Add a separate
visual method with a map or keyframes and failure analysis. For ATE/RPE, state definitions, units,
interval, alignment method, matched-sample fraction and route coverage. Tabulate available
sensors, reference data and condition differences. Incomparable scenes cannot establish sensor
superiority.
Find and explain examples of drift, degenerate scan matching, false closure, timing error and
sensor loss. Save settings for the valid run and modified versions. The map must contain
resolution, origin and frame_id, with unknown/free/occupied distinguished. Recalculating errors
must not require manual trajectory edits. Any component using pose must respond to stale data
and unconfirmed recovery.

Self-check
1. How does map differ from odom, and who publishes their transform? 2. Why does leg odometry
fail during slip? 3. What happens with an incorrect dt? 4. Why can an excessively small R worsen
EKF performance? 5. How does an unknown cell differ from a free one? 6. Why does scan matching
constrain some motion directions better than others?
7. How does a false loop edge change the whole trajectory? 8. What does a prior fix, and what does
it not measure? 9. How can ATE improve after failed frames are removed, and why does this distort
quality evaluation? 10. Why calculate RPE at several intervals? 11. When is scale correction
permissible? 12. Why does good SLAM not establish hexapod stability?

Handover to M16
Pass on the map, coordinate-frame chain, start and goal in one frame, an example localisation
status and a data-age limit. Add two maps: one valid and one with a known error or a passage
through an unknown region. The planner must reject a task when the pose estimate is unavailable.
A map alone does not establish whether an area is suitable as a foothold.
Demonstrate a normal circuit, a section with odometry error, a false closure and missing data.
Show numbers, then explain one failure cause without saying merely "poorly tuned". Frame the
final conclusion in terms of operating conditions: "in this scene, with these sensors and
parameters". Global navigation broadens the topics covered; local body and leg control can
develop using other state-estimation sources.

Assigned reading: a focused route through
the sections

The literature is mainly in English; the programme and definitions are also in English. Read the
selected sections before the relevant task, rather than entire books. Core reading is included in the
existing 35 analysis hours; consult references as needed.

Core reading
S. Thrun, W. Burgard, D. Fox, Probabilistic Robotics (2005), accompanying teaching
materials. Authors' slides: kalman.ppt - Bayesian filtering and EKF; mapping-occupancy.ppt -
occupancy updates; slam.ppt - joint map and trajectory estimation. Before Tasks 1-4, record states,
observations and assumptions. The MIT Press book provides detailed reference material on these
topics.
F. Dellaert, Factor Graphs and GTSAM: A Hands-on Introduction, teaching notes. Author's
GTSAM notes. §1 Factor Graphs, §3 Robot Localization, §4 PoseSLAM; for the prior example,
§2.2-2.4. In Tasks 5-6, build a small Pose2 graph with a prior and relative constraints, then add one
false closure.

Reference reading
R. Szeliski, Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications, 2nd ed. (2022). Author's book.
Ch. 11 Structure from motion and SLAM: selected material on pose estimation, bundle adjustment
and SLAM. For visual experiments in Tasks 5-7, connect correspondences, camera motion, error
accumulation and map correction.

Documentation for your experiment
ROS REP-105 - odom, map, Relationship between Frames. In Tasks 1-2, explicitly assign each TF
source and the location of global correction.
slam_toolbox - Parameters, Localization and serialization. For Task 4, learn to distinguish a saved
map image from a graph that can support continued operation.
evo: Metrics - Absolute pose error, Relative pose error, pose relation and alignment. Before Task 7,
choose units, time association and permissible trajectory alignment.

Minimum outcome from reading
Explain using your own route: where odom drifts, how map corrects global error, what connects two
poses in the graph, and why a plausible-looking map can conceal a false closure. Downloading
large amounts of KITTI and writing a second full SLAM system from scratch are not required.

Terms and definitions

Localisation. Estimating the robot's position and orientation relative to a known coordinate frame
or map using available measurements.
Odometry. Estimating relative motion from successive measurements; accumulated error usually
grows without external correction.
SLAM. Joint estimation of trajectory and map, linking motion uncertainty to uncertainty in
observed objects.
Prior. Information about the state before incorporating the current measurement; it may come
from a motion model or initial condition.
Likelihood. A function describing the probability of an observation for a given state and sensor
model; by itself, it is not a state distribution.
Posterior. The state distribution after combining prior information and a measurement under the
adopted probabilistic model.
EKF. A filter that approximates nonlinear models through local linearisation and represents its
estimate by a mean and covariance.
Innovation. The difference between a measurement and its prediction, interpreted with the
uncertainty of both prediction and sensor.
Covariance. A matrix of variances and joint deviations of estimate components; its units depend
on products of the corresponding component units.
odom frame. A local frame with a continuous trajectory that can gradually drift relative to the
surrounding world.
map frame. A global map frame that allows correction jumps when accumulated drift in the local
estimate is detected.
Occupancy grid. A map of spatial-cell occupancy probabilities; an unobserved cell must differ
from one confirmed free.
Log-odds. The logarithm of the probability ratio between an event and its absence; convenient for
occupancy updates.
Scan matching. Estimation of a transform from spatial measurements; it may be ambiguous with
sparse or repetitive geometry.
Pose graph. A representation of a trajectory as pose vertices and constraints between them,
obtained from motion or repeated observations.
Factor. A local likelihood or cost function relating a subset of unknown variables to a specific
measurement or condition.
Loop closure. A constraint between the current and a previously visited place, capable of
correcting drift if the correspondence is recognised correctly.
Gauge freedom. Ambiguity in the overall pose under relative constraints; removed by anchoring
or an absolute observation.
ATE. Error of estimated poses relative to reference poses after explicitly specified alignment; it
depends on time association and the selected component.
RPE. Relative-motion error between pose pairs over a declared temporal or spatial interval;
characterises local drift.

Motion fundamentals: support and odometry

Required: how a leg measures body motion
When the body moves above a stationary foot, joint angles change even though world-frame
contact velocity is zero. Encoders measure relative leg geometry; body velocity can be derived by
adding leg kinematics and IMU angular velocity. Before deriving the equations, check foot FK and
the cross product: rotation produces a linear velocity ω×r that depends on the lever arm.

From position to a velocity constraint
W is the world, B the body and F a fixed material point on the foot. p_WF=p_WB+R_WB p_BF(q); q
contains only leg joint angles in radians. J_p=∂p_BF/∂q is the 3×n position Jacobian in B, so relative
foot velocity is J_p q̇. This is not the foot's complete world-frame velocity.
Let v_WB be the linear velocity of the B origin expressed in W, and ω_B the body angular velocity
expressed in B. Differentiating gives v_WF=v_WB+R_WB(ω_B×p_BF+J_p q̇). All linear velocities are
in m/s; ω_B is in rad/s. Only a stationary, non-slipping support gives v_WF=0, hence
v_WB=−R_WB(ω_B×p_BF+J_p q̇). Contact alone does not justify setting this velocity to zero.

Example with body rotation
At one instant, R_WB=I, p_BF=(0.2; 0; −0.3) m, ω_B=(0; 0; 0.5) rad/s and J_p q̇=(−0.04; 0; 0) m/s.
The rotational contribution is ω_B×p_BF=(0; 0.10; 0) m/s. This gives v_WB=(0.04; −0.10; 0) m/s.
Omitting rotation loses the lateral motion of 0.10 m/s, even though FK and encoders are correct.
If the same foot actually slips at v_WF=(0.02; 0; 0) m/s, the true v_WB=(0.06; −0.10; 0) m/s. The
incorrect zero-velocity assumption gives an x-error of −0.02 m/s. Several reliable supports allow
residual comparisons; a shared geometry error or simultaneous slip can bias estimates from all
legs.

Exercises and evidence of understanding
1. Add a short synthetic stance episode to Task 1: calculate world-frame velocity in two ways, by
differentiating p_WF and by summing the three terms. Save a table and a residual plot; halving the
finite-difference step should reduce error until rounding error dominates. Ground truth is available
only to metric calculation, not to the state estimator.
2. In Task 2, repeat the example with slipping and loss of contact. Save the innovation and
support-admissibility flag; disabling an unreliable constraint must be visible. Explain why
integrating velocities produces drift and does not by itself determine absolute position and
orientation in map.
Time accounting: 1 hour from 1-2 for the leg-odometry model; 1 hour each from 13-14 and 15-16
for examples within Tasks 1-2. The total remains 75 hours; do not add physical walking or a new
filter beyond these tasks.
Assigned reading: Lynch/Park, Modern Robotics §3.2.2 Angular Velocities and §5.1.2 Body
Jacobian. Distinguish the book's six-dimensional spatial velocity (twist) from the point-position
Jacobian used here.

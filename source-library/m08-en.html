Geometry that turns a target into joint
angles

M8 • 02 May 2027 - 03 Jun 2027 • 75 hours

Outcome of the month
Create a verified kinematics library for a planar two-link arm and a leg with three degrees of
freedom. It will answer four questions: where the mechanism's endpoint lies for given angles,
which angles reach a target point, how joint velocities relate to endpoint velocity, and how an
external force relates to joint torques. This directly prepares for moving a walking robot's foot and
a fingertip. Deliver code, a geometry specification, visualisations, automated checks and clear
explanations of inverse-kinematics failure.

What you should already understand
Prerequisites from earlier months are vectors, matrices, trigonometry, derivatives, Python, NumPy
and tests. Bring the CAD leg's dimensions and axes from M5 and the available joint's sign, zero
offsets and angle ranges from M7. Before starting, manually evaluate sin and cos at 0, π/2 and π;
multiply a rotation matrix by a basis vector; convert millimetres to metres. Even if a numerical
library performs everything, explain the result's direction on a drawing. The main errors this month
usually arise from inconsistent conventions rather than difficult equations.
The first reference is a planar 2R mechanism: two revolute joints, lengths l1 and l2, and motion in
the x-y plane. The second is an exercise yaw-pitch-pitch leg: base rotation about z and two bends
in a radial vertical plane. If the M5 CAD model uses a different first joint axis, such as abduction,
write a separate transform chain from CAD; do not insert its dimensions into another mechanism's
formula. Reference models verify the mathematics, while adaptation to the actual leg must retain
its real axes and offsets.

What M8 does not require
Kinematics describes geometry, but does not establish sufficient torque, body stability or collision
freedom for every part. First calculate and visualise commands; permit transmission to a real
actuator only through M6-M7 limits and after a separate rig check. A complete gait planner, force
control and dynamic balance belong to later modules. For a hand, 2R can model two phalanges, but
tendon transmission and object contact require additional models.
At the end, check forward/inverse kinematics (FK/IK) consistency, verify the Jacobian numerically
and ensure predictable failure for unreachable targets. Declare numerical-test tolerances
separately from actual mechanical accuracy: a small computational residual does not remove
backlash, encoder-zero error or incorrect dimensions.

Project structure and 75 hours

Allocation across four blocks
Block A, 1-20: coordinate frames, rotations and transforms, 10 hours theory and 10 practice. Block
B, 21-40: link models and forward kinematics for 2R and 3-DOF, 10+10. Block C, 41-60: analytical
and numerical inverse kinematics, 10+10. Block D, 61-75: Jacobians, force, singularities and
acceptance checks, 5+10. Total: 35 hours of study and analysis, 40 hours of implementation and
experiments. Theory includes your own derivations on paper; practice includes code, plots, tests
and investigation of specific errors.
Divide ten-hour theory blocks into 2-hour sessions: one mechanism, one hand-worked example and
one software check. Schedule ten-hour practical blocks on Sundays and divide them into small
stages; breaks add to calendar time. Split the last 5 theory hours 2+2+1. Calendar boundaries
from the overall plan may overlap adjacent modules; retain rest days, holidays and integration
reviews. Allocate the hours below to available sessions without counting them again in the
neighbouring month.

Library organisation
Create geometry/ for transforms, models/ for 2R and the leg, solvers/ for IK, validation/ for
reference poses, visualizations/ for drawings and reports/ for results. Begin with a clear
Python/NumPy implementation. Add a C++ core only for a small, already verified interface if time
allows; a Rust port is optional. For every array, state shape, units and coordinate frame. Do not
give two different types of point the same name p without specifying their origin and the basis in
which they are expressed.

Conventions document
In conventions.md, record right-handed frames, body and joint axis directions, positive angle,
length and angle units, quaternion ordering, the meaning of T_AB, multiplication order and chosen
IK branch. Recommended convention: T_AB transforms coordinates from B to A, so p_A=T_AB·p_B
for a homogeneous point. Check every CAD export and library adapter against this document. Do
not fix an inverted leg by inserting an arbitrary minus sign before finding the cause.
For every experiment, save mechanism parameters, inputs, expected result and
random-configuration generator seed. Use error statuses UNREACHABLE, LIMIT_VIOLATION,
SINGULAR, NO_CONVERGENCE and INVALID_INPUT. Never return the final numerical approximation
as a successful command without verification: residual and status are part of the API result.

Coordinate frames and composition of
motion

What to study about transforms
Rotation R specifies basis orientation, and translation p locates its origin. A homogeneous matrix T
combines them: the upper block contains R and p, and the bottom row is [0,0,0,1]. For a pure
rotation, RᵀR=I and det(R)=+1. A matrix with det=-1 is a reflection, not an admissible rigid-body
rotation. Under the chosen convention, T_AC=T_AB·T_BC. Transform order matters: rotating a local
vector and then translating the result is generally different from reversing these operations.
Modern Robotics §3.3.1 establishes notation; follow frame indices rather than the words “left” and
“right”.

Task 1. Foot coordinates in different bases
Inputs: a drawing of body, hip and foot with simple known offsets. 1. Define T_body_hip and foot
coordinates in hip. 2. Calculate body-frame coordinates by hand. 3. Verify with the matrix in code.
4. Apply the inverse transform and recover the original point. Deliver an axis drawing and tests for
several poses, including a π/2 rotation. Check that a rigid transform preserves distance between
two points. Edge case: incorrect multiplication order must fail a test with nonzero translation and
rotation, even if the case without rotation appears correct.

Task 2. Translation of a point and a direction
The aim is to avoid adding hip position to a force or velocity vector. Take a contact point and force
direction in one frame. 1. Transform the point including translation. 2. Transform the free vector
using rotation only. 3. Compare results when base position changes without a change in
orientation. 4. Add separate transform_point and rotate_vector functions, or types that prevent
confusion. Save the test and illustration. Check that shifting the coordinate origin changes point
coordinates but not a free vector's components when the basis is unchanged.

Active and passive interpretations
A matrix may rotate a physical object or describe that same object in another coordinate frame;
either use needs consistent frame indices and inverse-matrix definitions. Draw two diagrams: body
rotation relative to the world, and a stationary world point expressed in the rotated body's frame.
Label what physically moves. For a leg, this explains why holding a foot fixed in the world while
tilting the body changes the local target point. Whole-robot IK is not yet needed; demonstrating the
correct transformation of one point is sufficient.

Rotations, quaternions and the mechanism
tree

Essential SO(2) and SO(3)
SO(2) describes planar rotations and SO(3) spatial rotations. Learn basic rotation matrices and
composition, then axis-angle orientation and a normalised quaternion. Euler angles are readable
but depend on axis order and have degenerate configurations. Quaternions q and -q describe the
same orientation; comparing components as ordinary points is insufficient. Record the chosen
order, for example [x,y,z,w] in your adapter, and check every external library's requirements,
including SciPy Rotation.

Task 3. Verify orientation representations
Use identity orientation, a π/2 rotation about each axis and a compound rotation. 1. Write expected
axis directions by hand. 2. Convert matrix to quaternion and back. 3. Check orthogonality,
determinant and angular error. 4. Supply q and -q and confirm the physical result is identical.
Deliver reference orientations and an adapter test. Edge cases: a zero quaternion, NaN and a
strongly non-unit input. The API must reject or normalise it according to an explicit policy, without
concealing invalidity.

Task 4. Coordinate-frame tree of the CAD leg
For each M5 part, specify parent, joint origin, axis, joint type, zero position and fixed offset. 1. Draw
the tree from body to foot. 2. Separate fixed mounting transforms from rotations q. 3. Assemble
the chain and visualise every local axis. 4. Check the zero pose and move each joint individually.
Save geometry in machine-readable form and images of at least four poses. Check that one joint
moves only its descendants; the body and parent links stay fixed. Error: an axis exported in world
coordinates must not be treated as local without transformation.

Two ways to describe one chain
Understand what a Denavit-Hartenberg table specifies and why standard and modified versions use
different conventions. An explicit transform chain matching CAD is convenient for the first
implementation. Then study the product of exponentials: fixed screw axes and the home
configuration define FK through a product of exponentials. Derive one planar revolute example and
compare with explicit matrices. Do not rewrite the entire project in three notations; the goal is to
read other models and recognise differences in conventions.

Forward kinematics of the arm and leg

Task 5. Planar 2R arm
For links l1,l2 and relative angles q1,q2 in the x-y plane, derive x=l1·cos(q1)+l2·cos(q1+q2),
y=l1·sin(q1)+l2·sin(q1+q2). The second link's world angle is q1+q2, not q2. 1. Derive the formula
from a drawing. 2. Implement analytical FK and an independent matrix chain. 3. Compare straight,
bent and folded poses. 4. Build a grid of permissible angles. Deliver two implementations and a
reachable-point map. Check that at q1=q2=0 the endpoint is (l1+l2,0); with equal lengths and
q2=π, it reaches the base. Handle zero and negative lengths according to geometry-validation
rules.

Task 6. Exercise leg with three axes
For yaw-pitch-pitch, define q1 about z and q2,q3 as angles in the vertical plane, with positive pitch
raising the link. Let l0 be a fixed radial offset and r=l0+l1·cos(q2)+l2·cos(q2+q3). Then
x=r·cos(q1), y=r·sin(q1), z=l1·sin(q2)+l2·sin(q2+q3). 1. Draw this exact mechanism. 2. Build an
independent transform chain. 3. Compare both forms at random permissible angles. 4. Add
T_body_hip and find foot coordinates in the body frame. Save a 3D view of the leg with axes and
tests. If CAD uses another mechanism, use its own chain and a separate reference model.

Comparison with CAD and a library
Choose at least five numerically specified poses and measure endpoint position in CAD or the
saved geometric representation. Compare values with your implementation and Robotics Toolbox
or an independent matrix implementation. State what is compared in the table: coordinates, units
and basis. A large error may arise from millimetres instead of metres, another joint zero, axis
ordering or a different point on the part. Find the cause one transform at a time from root to
endpoint, rather than compensating for the final error with an arbitrary offset.

Accuracy checks
For a purely numerical reference, choose tolerance in advance relative to mechanism length and
machine precision; for example, around 10⁻⁶ m may be a reasonable starting target for a
metre-scale model in double precision, but it is not the robot's mechanical accuracy. Check
geometry scaling and errors at boundary angles. Save the maximum norm of the difference
between implementations and the poses where it occurs. Random checks supplement, rather than
replace, hand-worked reference poses.

Inverse kinematics and solution branches

Analytical solution for the planar arm
For target (x,y), define c2=(x²+y²-l1²-l2²)/(2l1l2). If |c2| is substantially greater than 1, the target is
unreachable; a small round-off excess may be clipped only within the declared numerical
tolerance. For both branches, obtain q2=atan2(±sqrt(1-c2²),c2), then
q1=atan2(y,x)-atan2(l2·sin(q2),l1+l2·cos(q2)). Geometrically reachable points lie between circles
of radii |l1-l2| and l1+l2, but joint limits further reduce the workspace. Degenerate lengths and
folded configurations need explicit handling rules, rather than division by zero.

Task 7. Both branches and joint limits
1. Select a target with two permissible solutions. 2. Calculate both branches and check FK for each.
3. Add angle ranges from geometry and M7; remove solutions that violate them. 4. Choose a
solution close to the previous q while respecting the permitted range, rather than merely the
shortest angular difference around a circle. Deliver an API returning permissible solutions and a
failure reason. Check that FK(IK(p)) is close to p for all accepted points; IK(FK(q)) need not return
the original branch. Edge case: a target on the outer boundary where the two branches coincide.

Task 8. Foot targets for the 3-DOF leg
For the exercise yaw-pitch-pitch leg, first calculate q1=atan2(y,x), then solve the planar problem at
(sqrt(x²+y²)-l0,z). This branch assumes a selected radial-projection direction; the model must
explicitly bound additional configurations and ranges. 1. Transform the target from body to hip
coordinates. 2. Calculate solutions and check limits. 3. Verify them with full FK. 4. Follow a short
foot-lift-and-lower trajectory while retaining the selected branch. Save animation, angles and
maximum geometric residual. At x=y=0, yaw is undefined: return a special status or retain a
permissible previous yaw according to the interface rules.

A failure must not become a command
Distinguish no geometric solution, range violation and numerical failure. Return status,
q_candidates, residual, chosen branch and a diagnostic message. Do not silently replace an
unreachable point by the nearest one: projection must be a separate explicitly requested mode
returning the target actually reached. For a robot, the difference matters: an unnoticed change in
support point changes body position, and a fingertip may fail to reach the object.

Numerical IK and library design

Task 9. Iterative approximation
The aim is to solve a kinematic problem whose analytical formula is inconvenient and learn to
diagnose convergence. First use the familiar 2R model as a reference. Error e=p_target-FK(q).
Linearisation gives e≈J·Δq; solve the system or use a pseudoinverse instead of explicitly inverting a
matrix. For damped least squares, Δq=Jᵀ·(J·Jᵀ+λ²I)⁻¹e, where λ controls attenuation of large steps
near degeneracy. Implement with solve, a step limit and a finite iteration count. Do not present this
method as a guarantee of a global solution.
1. Begin with a central reachable target and several initial q values. 2. Record error norm and step
size at each iteration. 3. Add joint limits and a termination condition for lack of improvement. 4.
Compare with analytical IK on the same targets. Save convergence plots and a table of successful
and failed runs. Return SUCCESS only after checking final FK and limits. Edge cases: unreachable
target, poor initial seed, nearly straight arm and maximum iteration count. Do not return the last
approximation with a large residual as an executable command.

Unified robot-kinematics v1 interface
The model needs forward(q), inverse(target,seed,limits), jacobian(q), validate(q) and a geometry
description. Inputs must not be changed silently. forward returns position and, when required,
orientation with its basis specified. inverse returns a structured result rather than an unexplained
NaN array. Specify the input coordinate frame explicitly. Use one reference dataset for the
analytical implementation, numerical method and library comparison. A C++ wrapper may follow
these checks; porting to three languages is not the month's goal.

A small integration scenario
Place the body in several known poses and define one stationary world support point. Transform
that point to the hip frame, solve IK and recover world foot coordinates. Then repeat for the 2R
finger: a small target-point contour in its working plane. Plot target, reached point, angles, status
and distance to limits together. This checks geometric consistency across components. Velocities
and torques require separate calculations; a smooth-looking animation does not establish physical
feasibility of the profile.

Jacobians, forces and singular configurations

Task 10. Calculate foot velocity from joint velocities
For position FK, matrix J contains partial derivatives of coordinates with respect to q; each column
describes endpoint motion from a small motion of one joint. Linear velocity v=J(q)·q̇. Derive J for 2R
by hand, then obtain it for the leg analytically or using rotation axes and the vector to the foot
expressed in one coordinate frame. Begin only with the 2×2 or 3×3 position Jacobian; a spatial
velocity (twist) has 6 components and its Jacobian is 6×n. Specify linear/angular component
ordering separately.
Central-difference check: column i is approximately [FK(q+h·e_i)-FK(q-h·e_i)]/(2h). Compare several
h values, for example 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁶ rad, at random and boundary poses. Save maximum
error norm and a plot against step size. An excessively small h amplifies round-off, while an
excessively large h increases linear-approximation error. Then specify q̇ and compare predicted
endpoint velocity with a small actual FK step. Edge case: derivative expressed in the wrong basis
with a nonzero base rotation.

Task 11. Foot force and joint torque
Fix the meaning of F: external force applied at the mechanism's endpoint, expressed in the same
basis as J. The corresponding generalised torque is τ=JᵀF; to balance this load, the actuator
generally provides the opposite contribution while accounting for other forces. 1. Check one joint
using r×F. 2. Compare with JᵀF for the arm. 3. Repeat for the leg and several force directions. 4.
Check virtual work, τᵀδq≈Fᵀδp. Deliver a static-load calculation and sign test. The formula itself
excludes link mass, acceleration and friction; M9 adds these.

Task 12. Map proximity to singularity
A singularity is a loss of Jacobian rank relative to its normal maximum. For 2R, compare bent and
fully straight poses. Over a grid, calculate the smallest singular value and available velocity
directions; state normalisation and units explicitly. Request motion along the direction lost in the
straight pose. Save the map and explain why a small target displacement may require a large Δq.
Add status checks and step limits to IK. Proximity to singularity does not itself mean collision, but
requires special command-handling rules.

Acceptance, negative tests and self-check

Required scenarios
F1: rotation and translation applied in reverse order. The task 1 reference pose detects it. F2:
millimetres treated as metres. Scale checking and a known length reveal the discrepancy; relative
error between functions sharing the same mistake must not produce a false PASS. F3: target
beyond the outer radius or inside the unreachable inner circle of 2R. Return UNREACHABLE. F4:
geometrically reachable point requires a forbidden angle. Return LIMIT_VIOLATION. F5: fully
straight arm. The Jacobian loses rank and IK must not return an unbounded jump as a permissible
command.
F6: two neighbouring requests each have two solution branches. The continuity policy retains a
permissible branch or reports the need to switch. F7: poor seed for numerical IK. At the iteration
limit, return NO_CONVERGENCE with residual. F8: NaN, zero length or zero quaternion. Reject input
according to interface rules. F9: q and -q. The orientation check treats them as equivalent. F10:
force expressed in a different basis. The virtual-work test detects inconsistency. F11: incorrect
encoder-zero sign. Testing the joint adapter in a known calibration configuration reveals the error.

Completion conditions
Both models have documented geometry and automated tests. For accepted IK solutions, the norm
of FK(q)-target lies within the declared numerical tolerance and joint angles remain in range. State
maximum Jacobian error relative to numerical differentiation with the method, steps and pose set.
Include hand-worked references, random checks and independent comparison; two functions with
a shared error do not sufficiently validate each other. Unreachable and singular requests return
clear status, and reproducing results does not require manual file corrections.

Review questions and handover to M9
Explain what T_AB means; why a point and force transform differently; why 2R q2 is relative; why
IK need not recover the same branch; how numerical convergence is defined; why rank matters
more than one large J component; what sign the torque balancing an external force must have; and
how geometry error differs from backlash. Show answers in your drawings and plots. Pass lengths,
local axes, zero offsets, FK, Jacobians and permissible configurations to M9. Masses, centres of
mass, inertias and contacts will be added separately; do not conceal their absence behind a
successful kinematic animation.

Assigned reading: a focused reading route

Books and notes may be in English; keep the plan, definitions and your explanations in English for
this edition. Read only the specified sections within the existing 75 hours.

Core reading and one reference route
1. Core: K. Lynch, F. Park, Modern Robotics. §3.3.1 homogeneous transforms; §4.1.1 product of
exponentials; §5.1-5.3 Jacobian, statics and singularities; §6.2 numerical IK. This is the route for
tasks 1-12: read the relevant section before deriving equations yourself.
2. Reference: S. LaValle, Planning Algorithms. §3.2 Rigid-Body Transformations and §3.3
Transforming Kinematic Chains. For tasks 1-4, compare the geometric explanation of transforms
and chains; one 2D chain and the transition to 3D are sufficient.
3. Core, selectively: R. Tedrake, Robotic Manipulation: Basic Pick and Place. Named sections
Forward kinematics and Differential inverse kinematics. For tasks 9-10, connect endpoint
coordinates with joint velocities; subsequent motion planning is outside this module.

Documentation for the practical work
SciPy Rotation - apply, inv, from_quat and as_quat; check component ordering and q/-q
equivalence in task 3.

Glossary: terms and definitions

Coordinate frame. An origin and ordered axes relative to which point coordinates or
physical-vector components are expressed.
Pose. A combined description of body position and orientation relative to an explicitly named
coordinate frame.
Rotation matrix. A matrix R preserving lengths and basis handedness; for spatial rotation, RᵀR=I
and det(R)=1.
Homogeneous transform. A 4×4 matrix combining rotation and translation of a point between
two specified coordinate frames.
Composition. Sequential application of transforms; frame indices determine matrix multiplication
order, which generally affects the result.
Degree of freedom. An independent parameter needed to specify a mechanism's configuration
under its geometric constraints.
Configuration. A set of independent mechanism coordinates; for the open chain considered here,
the joint-angle vector q.
Kinematic chain. Rigid links connected by joints whose relative transforms determine the position
of each successive link.
Home configuration. A predefined configuration at zero joint coordinates, serving as the
geometric reference.
Joint axis. An oriented geometric line of rotation or direction of translation, expressed in a
specified coordinate frame.
Forward kinematics. Calculating a selected link's pose from joint configuration and fixed
mechanism geometry.
Inverse kinematics. Finding configurations that achieve a specified pose or point, accounting for
joint limits and solution accuracy.
IK branch. One of several distinct configurations or continuous configuration families that achieve
the same target pose.
Workspace. The set of reachable points or poses of a selected mechanism endpoint under
specified geometry and joint limits.
Quaternion. A four-component, unit-norm orientation representation; quaternions of opposite sign
describe the same rotation.
Jacobian. A matrix relating joint velocities locally to selected endpoint coordinates; its dimensions
and units depend on the task.
Singularity. A configuration with loss of Jacobian rank in which some endpoint-motion directions
become locally unavailable.
Pseudoinverse. A generalisation of matrix inversion for linear least-squares problems that selects
a minimum-norm solution.
Damped least squares IK. A regularised local solution that suppresses excessive joint-coordinate
changes near degeneracy at the cost of residual error.
Check the relationships: ṗ=J(q)q̇; with consistent signs, the generalised force from an external
action is JᵀF. Do not confuse it with a holding command.

Motion fundamentals: from a point to joints

Required worked example: FK → IK → velocity → force
Use two rigid links in the planar part of a leg with a fixed base. x points right, y up and +z towards
the viewer; angles are anticlockwise. Measure q1 from +x and q2 relative to the first link. Check
200 mm=0.2 m. Here v=q̇, in rad/s; endpoint velocity ṗ, in m/s, is a different quantity.
For l1=l2=0.2 m, add link vectors: x=0.2cosq1+0.2cos(q1+q2), y=0.2sinq1+0.2sin(q1+q2). At
q=(0;π/2), endpoint p=(0.2;0.2) m. FK applies known rotations in sequence; IK solves the inverse
geometric problem. The cosine rule gives cosq2=(x²+y²-l1²-l2²)/(2l1l2)=0. The branches are (0;π/2)
and (π/2;-π/2); both reach the point, but joint limits may exclude one.

The Jacobian: local motion and its limits
Rotation of joint i moves the endpoint tangentially. Thus a position-Jacobian column is z_i×(p-o_i):
axis z_i and the vector from joint o_i to the endpoint are expressed in one basis. At our pose,
J=[[-0.2; -0.2]; [0.2; 0]] m/rad. With v=(0.5;-0.25) rad/s, ṗ=Jv=(-0.05;0.10) m/s. This is an
instantaneous relation; a finite path requires recalculating J as q changes.
At the straight pose q=(0;0), both tangents are vertical, the x row of J is zero and rank is 1.
Instantaneous velocity along x is unavailable, although a bent chain can change x. This is a
singularity: a small requested point displacement does not guarantee small joint velocities. IK step
limiting and damping do not create the missing direction.

Force uses the same geometry; dynamics comes later
In a separate static experiment, apply f_ext=(0;-5) N at the endpoint: this is the environment's
force on the robot. With link masses and friction excluded, τ_ext=Jᵀf_ext=(-1;0) N·m, and holding
requires τ_act=-τ_ext=(1;0) N·m. Torque at the second joint is zero because the force's line of
action passes through its axis. Link weight and acceleration change the requirement according to
the M9 model; successful IK does not establish motion feasibility.

One check connects tasks 5, 7 and 10-12
Save a drawing of both branches, a p/J value table and tests. Compare J columns with central
differences of FK at h=10⁻⁴ and 10⁻⁶ rad, then repeat at the straight pose. Independently calculate
each torque using r×f_ext. Check power at the stated v: f_extᵀṗ=τ_extᵀv=-0.5 W. This explains the
transpose of J and the external-action sign; it is a calculation, not a torque command to a
position-controlled servo.

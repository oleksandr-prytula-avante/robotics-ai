Mathematics of robot state and motion

M2 • 03 Nov 2026 - 29 Nov 2026 • 75 hours

What you should achieve by the end of M02
Learn to turn a simple leg or finger problem into quantities, equations and a verifiable calculation.
For example: find a force's moment arm from a support point's position; recover an angle from
encoder counts; obtain velocity from a trajectory; predict a compliant support's motion from mass,
stiffness and damping. The main deliverable is robot-math, a collection of numerical and
geometric checks reusable in later modules.
The scope is deliberately limited: vectors and matrices, linear systems and least squares,
derivatives and integrals, and ordinary differential equations. Introduce the Jacobian as a table of
sensitivities and eigenvalues as properties of a simple linear transformation and system. Do not yet
implement full 3D robot kinematics, spatial velocities, controllers or a Kalman filter.

Starting model and preparation
Use Python, NumPy, Matplotlib and SciPy in the M01 environment. Create notebooks/01_vectors,
02_calibration, 03_motion and 04_odes; move repeated functions into robot_math/. Each notebook
must contain a problem statement, axes diagram, parameters with units, calculation, error plot and
conclusion. Keep raw and computed data separately; pin dependency versions and the
random-number generator seed.
The shared teaching object is a link of length L=0.12 m with one revolute joint and a support point
at its tip. Masses, forces and other numbers in this document are calculation-example parameters,
not authorisation to test a future design. The second object is a compliant support with mass
m=0.5 kg, stiffness k=50 N/m and viscous damping coefficient c=1 N·s/m. M02 investigates motion
only through calculation.

Entry diagnostic: within the first two hours
Without references, convert 120 mm to metres, 90° to radians and 10 ms to seconds; find sin(π/2),
a rectangle's area and the solution of two linear equations. In Python, create an array of shape
(100,3), select one coordinate and calculate its mean. If unit arithmetic or trigonometry is difficult,
spend up to 90 minutes of block A addressing that specific gap and reduce decorative plotting
work.
Starting criterion: explain in your own words the difference between a number, a vector component
and a physical quantity. Record one M01 error that mathematics could detect: a reversed axis sign,
milliseconds used instead of seconds, or a false velocity jump from a missing packet. This becomes
your first regression example.

Allocating 75 hours and recording
calculations

Four study blocks
A - hours 1-20: 10 h of theory and hand calculations, 10 h of practice. Topics: units, vectors,
bases, dot and cross products, matrices and rank. Deliverable: force diagrams and a library of
verifiable point and vector transformations.
B - hours 21-40: 10 h of theory and 10 h of practice. Topics: Gaussian elimination,
overdetermined systems, residuals, QR, conditioning and eigenvalues. Deliverable: encoder
calibration with a separate validation set and an unsuccessful experiment example.
C - hours 41-60: 10 h of theory and 10 h of practice. Topics: derivatives, integrals, partial
derivatives, gradients and an introductory Jacobian. Deliverable: link-trajectory analysis, work done
by a force and sensitivity of the endpoint position to angle.
D - hours 61-75: 5 h of theory and 10 h of practice. Topics: initial-value problems, explicit Euler,
semi-implicit Euler, RK4, stability and step-size error. Deliverable: mass-spring-damper, pendulum
and convergence report. Total: exactly 35 h of analysis and 40 h of practice.

Session rhythm
Divide each 10 h of theory into five two-hour sessions: 15 min reviewing the preceding idea, 35 min
reading, 40 min hand calculations, 20 min Python verification and 10 min recording conclusions.
Theory in the final block takes 2+2+1 h. Divide the ten-hour practical into five 2 h sessions:
formulation, implementation, cross-checking, error analysis and presentation. Breaks are outside
study hours.
The calendar window on the first page comes from the roadmap. A boundary date may belong to
two modules; rest days, holidays and integration stages remain as in the original plan. Numbered
work steps fill available sessions sequentially without recounting hours. Do not add an extra hour to
every module.

When to move from reading to practice
After each section, close the text and explain which quantity you are calculating, why the operation
is valid and how you will verify the answer. One correct example and one deliberately faulty
example are more useful than a second lecture on the same topic. Use these log fields: robot task;
model; assumptions; prediction before running; result; error; next check.
If behind schedule, first remove animation styling and a second language implementation, then
reduce parameter sweeps. Retain four notebooks, both dynamic objects, unit checks and method
comparisons. Full QR proofs and derivation of RK4 from Taylor series can wait; explanations of
residuals, time steps and causes of discrepancies are compulsory.

Vectors, bases and forces on a link

Topics to study
Study point and free-vector coordinates, length and normalisation, force addition, bases and
projections. The dot product a·b connects components to angle; for unit direction n, the force
projection is F·n. The cross product r×F gives torque about a chosen origin. Measure r in metres, F
in newtons and torque in N·m; its direction follows the right-hand rule.
Study matrix-vector multiplication as a coordinate transformation, matrix product order,
transposition and the meaning of an inverse matrix. For planar rotation, use R(θ) with first row
[cosθ, −sinθ] and second row [sinθ, cosθ]. State the convention explicitly: an active anticlockwise
vector rotation in a fixed frame. The reverse transformation uses R(θ)ᵀ; do not confuse the two
operations.

Task 1. Loading a finger or shank
Input: r=(0.12;0;0) m, F=(0;0;−5) N. First draw a side view and predict the torque direction. Then
calculate r×F by hand and with NumPy, find its magnitude and project F onto the link axis. Rotate
the force by 30° in the same plane and repeat. Deliverable: diagram and table of four positions,
labelling axes, units and signs.
Check: in the first case, torque is (0;0.6;0) N·m; collinear r and F give zero. Doubling the
moment-arm length must double torque magnitude. Faulty case: enter 120 instead of 0.12 and
explain the exact thousandfold discrepancy. This checks the unit model; it is not a physical motor
test.

Task 2. Sensor coordinates on a rotated body
Input: three unit vectors and ten arbitrary points in the plane. Implement R and rotate the set by
0°, 90°, 180° and back. For an offset-mounted sensor, distinguish p_body=R·p_sensor+t from the
direction transformation v_body=R·v_sensor. Do not add translation t to force, acceleration or
direction.
Save a plot of original and transformed axes, a norm test and a reverse-transformation test. For
float64 numbers of order one, use a teaching absolute tolerance of 10⁻¹²; also explain the scale
choice. Test the zero vector without normalising it and an angle in degrees mistakenly passed as
radians. Be able to state by hand the result of rotating (1,0) by 90°.

Linear systems and encoder calibration

From equation to solution
Study A·x=b: each row defines a constraint and each column an unknown's contribution. Work
through Gaussian elimination on a 2×2 system, row swaps for a poor pivot, and nonexistent or
nonunique solutions. Rank is the number of independent constraints. The inverse matrix is useful
conceptually; solve numerically with solve or lstsq rather than inverse(A)·b.
For calibration q=a·u+b, u is the encoder count and q is in radians. Construct A from a column of u
values and a column of ones. With more observations than parameters, minimise the sum of
squared residuals r=A·x−q. QR separates the problem into an orthogonal transformation and a
triangular system; understand this role and one 3×2 example without writing a general QR
implementation.

Task 3. A teaching body with two supports
A 2 kg body rests on two points at horizontal positions x=0 and x=0.3 m; its centre of mass is at
x=0.12 m. Write vertical force and torque equilibrium equations; find support reactions by hand
and in Python. With g=9.81 m/s², reactions must sum to 19.62 N, and opposing moments about the
left support must balance.
Move the centre of mass from 0 to 0.3 m. A negative reaction outside the segment means the
contact model, which can only push, is invalid. Do not present it as a feasible leg force. Save a
reaction-force plot and mark the model's validity boundary; balance control comes later.

Task 4. Find encoder scale and zero
Create 21 u positions evenly spanning the teaching range −1000…1000. Generate q with known
a=0.001 rad/count, b=0.03 rad and small noise with fixed settings; save the ground-truth
parameters separately. Reserve some points for validation. Estimate the parameters, plot residual
against u and calculate RMSE=sqrt(mean(r²)) in radians. First check noise-free data: parameters
must be recovered to machine precision.
Then restrict inputs to around u=500 and compare parameter sensitivity to a small change in one
observation. A small error within a narrow region does not establish good calibration across the full
range. Deliverables: coefficients.json, raw CSV, training and held-out error plots, rank of A and an
explanation of units. Failure cases: identical u values, an empty set, NaN and a duplicate
mistakenly counted as a new independent measurement.

Conditioning and eigenvector directions

Skills needed before M03 optimisation
The condition number describes how much input error can be amplified in the answer for a
specified norm and scale. A large value warrants examining the formulation, units and data
diversity. It is not a universal good/bad switch: parameters in millimetres and metres have different
scales. Compare normalised formulations and always show the actual change in the answer.
Under a matrix transformation, an eigenvector remains on the same line; its eigenvalue is the
multiplier. For a diagonal stiffness matrix K=diag(40,100) N/m, the directions are obvious. If
off-diagonal terms couple the axes, compute the directions. For now, use a symmetric 2×2 matrix
and check K·v=λ·v; leave complex modes, spectral control and general multilink mechanics for
later months.

Task 5. Nearly identical support measurements
Use A=[[1,1],[1,1+ε]] and b generated from known x=(1,2). Set ε=10⁻¹, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁸. For each case,
save cond(A), the residual and the error in recovered x. Add the same small relative perturbation to
one b component and repeat. This illustrates two nearly duplicate constraints when determining a
robot's state.
Check: a small residual can coexist with a large x error. Demonstrate this numerically, then replace
the second equation with an independent one and repeat. Deliverable: six-run table and a
conclusion identifying an additional measurement that resolves the problem. Do not insert a
pseudoinverse without explanation: it belongs to M03 and cannot create missing information.

Task 6. Stiff and compliant support directions
For K=[[80,20],[20,50]] N/m, use eigh to find eigenvalues and normalised eigenvectors. Draw
original axes and eigenvector directions. Apply an equal teaching force along each direction and
find displacement from K·x=F. A greater stiffness λ produces less displacement for the same force
magnitude.
Check orthogonality of the two directions, each eigen-equation's residual and reconstruction of the
original matrix. Set stiffness along one axis to zero: this model no longer determines static
displacement along the free direction. Save it as an expected solve failure caused by a singular
system. Additional 20-minute experiment: rotate K using orthogonal R and verify unchanged
eigenvalues.

Derivatives, integrals and motion sensitivity

Theory connected to motion
The time derivative of position is velocity; the derivative of velocity is acceleration. For angle q,
units are rad, rad/s and rad/s². Study derivatives of sums, products and sin/cos, and the chain rule
for q(t)=A·sin(ωt), with ω in rad/s. Integrating velocity gives coordinate change; integrating force
along a path gives work in joules, and over time gives impulse in N·s. Label the integration variable
for every integral.
A partial derivative describes how a function changes when one argument varies and the others
remain fixed. The gradient collects these derivatives for a scalar function; a vector function's
Jacobian arranges them with outputs in rows and inputs in columns. Do not call every
multidimensional array a Jacobian: first specify the function, input/output dimensions and each
element's units.

Task 7. Encoder velocity and noise
Generate 5 s of motion q=0.4·sin(2πt) rad with a 0.01 s step. Derive exact velocity and
acceleration. Implement forward and central differences; compare central differences only at
interior samples and state the time assigned to each forward-difference estimate. Add angle noise
and plot velocity error. Compare two time steps with both noise-free and noisy input.
Save q, dq/dt and absolute-error plots with RMSE over the same time window. Noise-free error
should decrease for sufficiently small steps, while differentiating a noisy signal too frequently can
worsen the estimate. Remove one sample and use actual timestamps: division by constant dt at
that point becomes a deliberate error case. M04 covers filtering in detail.

Task 8. Link-endpoint sensitivity
For p(q)=(L·cos q,L·sin q), derive J(q)=(−L·sin q,L·cos q)ᵀ. With L=0.12 m and q=30°, estimate
Δp≈J·Δq for Δq=0.1°, 1°, 10°. Compare with exact p(q+Δq)−p(q); the error magnitude shows
where the linear approximation loses accuracy. Deliverables: tangential-displacement diagram and
error table in millimetres.
Separately integrate a known velocity with the trapezoidal rule and check that the angle returns
after a full period. Calculate work done by a constant torque of 0.2 N·m through 30°: W=τ·Δq, with
angle in radians. Do not confuse torque with energy even though both have dimensional product
N·m: their physical meanings and operations differ.

ODEs: a compliant leg and a free pendulum

Formulating an initial-value problem
For a spring-supported mass with displacement measured from equilibrium, m·x″+c·x′+k·x=F(t).
Introduce v=x′ and state y=(x,v); then x′=v and v′=(F−c·v−k·x)/m. Initial values x(0) and v(0) are
compulsory parts of the problem. Measuring from equilibrium removes constant gravity; with
another coordinate definition, explicitly restore gravity with the correct sign.
Explicit Euler evaluates the derivative at the step's start. Semi-implicit Euler updates v first, then
uses the new v to update x. RK4 evaluates four intermediate derivatives; implement it for general
y′=f(t,y). It has a fixed step and fourth-order accuracy for sufficiently smooth problems; library
RK45 uses a different scheme and adaptive control, so it is not the same algorithm.

Task 9. Mass-spring-damper
Use the parameters on page 1, x(0)=0.02 m, v(0)=0 and F=0, for 5 s. Implement the three
methods and a reference solve_ivp calculation with stricter tolerances. For each method, test steps
0.04; 0.02; 0.01 s and compare x and v at common times. In a separate undamped experiment
with c=0, track E=mv²/2+kx²/2.
Save state, energy and error-versus-step plots. For c>0 without external force, energy must
decrease in the continuous model. Energy growth may indicate numerical instability or a sign error.
A decay plot alone is insufficient: check equilibrium x=v=0 and compare with the analytical
solution for c=0.

Task 10. Pendulum model of a free link
Set θ″=−(g/L)·sinθ, L=0.3 m, θ(0)=0.1 rad, ω(0)=0. Measure the period between zero crossings in
the same direction and compare with T≈2π·sqrt(L/g). Increase the initial angle to 1 rad and
demonstrate the small-angle approximation's limitation. Energy E=mL²ω²/2+mgL(1−cosθ) must
remain constant in the ideal continuous model.
Test θ=0, a negative initial angle and an excessively large step. Reject negative m or L before
integration. Save solver settings and an error estimate; agreement between two equally faulty
implementations cannot replace an independent analytical reference. Actual foot impacts, joint
friction and backlash are not yet part of this model.

The shared robot-math test bench and error
checks

Connect all notebooks through one scenario
Scenario: a virtual encoder produces u(t), calibration converts it to q(t), geometry computes the
link endpoint, a numerical derivative gives velocity, and an independent pendulum model supplies
reference motion. Define each stage's input/output contract. Data include t_s, encoder_count,
q_rad, p_x_m and p_z_m; settings include L_m, a_rad_per_count and b_rad.
First generate a trajectory from the ODE, then convert q to u through inverse calibration and
quantisation. The analyser must recover motion with the expected precision loss. Combine existing
functions; a separate large project is unnecessary. In the report, distinguish model, quantisation,
calibration and numerical-differentiation errors: the same final RMSE can have different causes.

Six required scenarios
1. Wrong units. Pass a length in millimetres to an interface expecting metres; a schema or scale
check must explain the failure. 2. Zero vector. Normalisation must return a clear error rather than
propagate NaN through the remaining calculation. 3. Singular calibration. All u values are equal;
the report identifies insufficient rank and does not produce an unsupported scale coefficient.
4. Poor conditioning. Nearly identical equations give a sensitive answer; the programme must
not present a small residual as proof of accuracy. 5. Missing time sample. A removed packet
must not cause an artificial doubling of velocity through a hard-coded dt. 6. Unstable step.
Detect energy growth in an ideal spring and reduce it by adjusting the method or step.

Numerical and geometric checks
For exact noise-free examples, combine absolute and relative tolerances: |a−b|≤atol+rtol·|b|.
Absolute tolerance matters near zero; relative tolerance matters when scale changes. Choose
tolerances before viewing results and label their units. For integration methods, the main check is
convergence over a series of steps, rather than one fortuitously accurate value.
In test_report.csv, record test name, input scenario, expectation, observation, threshold and
pass/fail. Expected failures are valid: a test passes if the programme recognises an invalid
formulation. Save at least one plot each of a faulty and corrected version. For the final run, remove
computed results and recreate them from raw data using one recorded command sequence.

Acceptance checks and handover to later
modules

What to save in robot-math
Four completed notebooks; a function module with clear units; input CSVs; parameter
configurations; calibration coefficients; test table; norm, residual, derivative and energy plots; and
README with execution order. State the formula and seed for synthetic data; store reference
values separately. Label the 'raw measurements' field synthetic until a real sensor is used.
Begin each notebook with a robotics question and end with an engineering conclusion. 'The code
ran' is not a conclusion. Suitable statements include: 'the length-scale error increased torque a
thousandfold'; 'the narrow encoder range prevents stable scale estimation'; 'the chosen Euler step
increases this oscillator's energy'. Fill in all numerical results after your own run.

Passing criteria
1. Each of ten practical tasks has an input, result, independent check and edge case. 2.
Parameters, columns and plot axes have units. 3. Analytical and numerical answers are compared
over the same time interval. 4. ODE results cover at least three step sizes and both objects. 5.
Singularity and instability are explicitly identified. 6. A clean run reproduces conclusions without
manually editing intermediate files.
Passing many tests does not replace understanding. For the oral review, choose six questions
below at random and answer each with a small diagram or calculation. You may consult NumPy
syntax; explain the underlying torque, linear-calibration and ODE formulae independently.

Self-check questions
Why does shifting the coordinate origin affect a point but not a direction? When is r×F zero? What
does a negative support reaction mean? How does an overdetermined system differ from an
inconsistent one? Why are residual and coefficient error different quantities? How do units affect
numerical conditioning?
How do you obtain link-endpoint velocity from joint velocity? Why does numerical differentiation
amplify noise? What is a 'linear approximation in angle'? Which initial conditions does a pendulum
require? Why is energy conservation useful as a test? How does fixed-step RK4 differ from RK45 in
solve_ivp?

Subsequent use
M03 uses the same calibration and Jacobian for uncertainty estimation. M04 uses the noisy encoder
and derivative to study filtering. M05 uses torque, reactions and eigenvector directions as
preparation for mechanics. Preserve the functions' public interface, but do not claim real-robot
accuracy from synthetic data. Physical test-bench validation follows electronics and mechanical
preparation.

Assigned reading: a short route

Read only the specified passages before the corresponding experiment. References are mainly in
English; keep explanations, glossary and report in English. Reading and preparation fit within the
existing 75 hours; whole books are not required.

Core reading
1. Gilbert Strang, MIT 18.06: Linear Algebra. Lectures 1-2, 16, 21. Equation geometry and
elimination support reaction calculations; projections and least squares support calibration;
eigenvector directions support task 6. After each section, solve one 2×2 example by hand.

Glossary: terms and definitions

Vector. A geometric quantity with direction and magnitude; its components depend on the chosen
basis, while the quantity itself does not.
Basis. A set of linearly independent vectors in terms of which every vector in the space has a
unique representation.
Coordinate frame. An origin together with a basis; point coordinates change when the origin
shifts or the axes rotate.
Rotation matrix. A matrix describing an orthonormal basis's orientation; a proper rotation
satisfies RᵀR=I and det R=1.
Cross product. In three dimensions, a vector perpendicular to both operands; its direction follows
the right-hand rule and its magnitude is the parallelogram's area.
Torque. The vector r×F about a chosen point; it describes a force's rotational effect and is
measured in N·m.
Rank. Number of independent matrix columns or rows; it determines how many independent
constraints a linear system contains.
Residual. The difference A·x−b for a proposed solution; a small residual alone does not guarantee
accurate unknown parameters.
Least squares. Choosing parameters to minimise the sum of squared residuals; fits a model to
redundant, usually inconsistent measurements.
Conditioning. Sensitivity of a problem's result to small input changes; depends on its formulation,
units and scaling.
Eigenvector direction. Direction of a nonzero vector v satisfying A·v=λv. The matrix scales the
vector while preserving the line on which it lies.
Derivative. Limit of the ratio of function increment to argument increment; differentiating position
with respect to time gives velocity in m/s.
Integral. Accumulated contribution of a function over an interval; integrating velocity over time
gives displacement with consistent initial conditions.
Jacobian. Matrix of partial derivatives of output coordinates with respect to inputs; approximately
maps small input changes to output changes.
Ordinary differential equation (ODE). Equation relating an unknown function of one variable to
its derivatives; a motion model also requires initial conditions.
Time step. Time interval for one numerical state update; affects trajectory error, stability and
computation cost.
Numerical stability. Property of a numerical scheme in which perturbations do not grow
unacceptably for the problem and chosen integration step.

Motion fundamentals: from joint angle to
foot velocity

Compulsory, within 75 hours. Use 1 h from session 49-50 and 2 h of practice 51-60 already
allocated to p(q) sensitivity and checks. This connects tasks 2 and 8; it does not add tasks beyond
the plan. Before starting, convert 30° to π/6 rad and explain why m/s differs from rad/s.

Why joint velocity differs from point velocity
Consider a rigid link of length L=0.12 m with one revolute joint. Frame A is fixed: its origin is on the
joint axis, x points right, y upwards, and positive q is anticlockwise from x. The foot point has
coordinates p_A(q)=(L cos q, L sin q). Length is constant; backlash and deformation are excluded.
This requires geometry, not link mass.
For a small rotation, endpoint displacement is approximately tangent to the circle. By the chain
rule, v_A=dp_A/dt=J(q)·q̇, where J=(−L sin q, L cos q)ᵀ, in m/rad. Thus |v_A|=L|q̇| and p_A·v_A=0.
The Jacobian depends on configuration: equal angular velocities at different q give equal endpoint
speed but different directions. Even at constant q̇, velocity changes direction: acceleration
a_A=−p_A·q̇² points towards the joint. This last equality assumes q̈=0.

Numerical example and change of fixed frame
At q=π/6 rad and q̇=0.5 rad/s, p_A=(0.103923; 0.060000) m, v_A=(−0.030000; 0.051962) m/s and
|v_A|=0.060000 m/s. Over a short time Δt=0.01 s, tangential displacement is approximately 0.6
mm; distinguish distance travelled in this interval from a change in one coordinate.
Let R_BA=[[0,−1],[1,0]] describe A's axes in another fixed frame B, and t_BA=(0.20;0.10) m be A's
origin position in B. Then p_B=R_BA·p_A+t_BA=(0.140000;0.203923) m, but
v_B=R_BA·v_A=(−0.051962;−0.030000) m/s. The derivative of a constant translation is zero. A
moving B introduces additional terms; that case is not modelled here.

1. In robot-math, compute the central difference [p(q+q̇h)−p(q−q̇h)]/(2h), h=10⁻⁴ s, and compare
with Jq̇. For these noise-free float64 values, use a teaching tolerance of 10⁻⁸ m/s on the error norm.
Save a table for q=0, π/6, π/2; check tangency, speed magnitude and inverse coordinate
transformation.
2. On the leg diagram, label angle, both frames and the v arrow. Predict doubled speed when L
doubles and a reversed arrow when q̇ changes sign. Deliverables: one drawing and tests for tasks
2/8. These check kinematics; they do not establish the existence of a motor capable of producing
the motion.

§3.3.1, changing coordinate frames. Spatial velocities are studied in M08.

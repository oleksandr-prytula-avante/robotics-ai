Monthly outcome: paths, trajectories and
behaviour

M16 • 21 Jan 2028 - 15 Feb 2028 • 75 hours

Prepare comparative planner tests and a small autonomous task with a behaviour tree. The robot
receives a goal, finds a valid path, builds a speed-limited trajectory along it and monitors
execution, explicitly responding when it cannot continue. This month's examples are a body route
among obstacles and a manipulator approach to an object. These foundations precede
foot-placement planning.

Three different questions
Search determines which states connect the start and goal. A geometric path q(s) describes a
configuration sequence without time. A trajectory q(t) adds time, velocity and acceleration. Task
logic decides when to plan, wait, cancel and recover. A* success does not mean an actuator can
execute the path; successful arm planning does not mean the object has already been grasped.

What to use from earlier months
Use the M15 map with unknown cells, M14 scene geometry, and link kinematics and actuator limits
from earlier modules. Check that start and goal use one frame and that angular and linear units are
consistent. The prerequisites are reading URDF, running ROS 2 actions, constructing a graph and
explaining a derivative. Review how a joint-angle trajectory produces rad/s and rad/s².

Two small experimental setups
Setup A is a 2D room map and a body with a finite planar footprint. You may use a simulated
wheeled base to learn Nav2. Setup B is a ready-made manipulator model in MoveIt 2, with a table
and a box. Choose one prepared package for each, compatible with Ubuntu 24.04 and ROS 2 Jazzy.
Do not build a complex robot model and a new environment from scratch this month.
The final package contains your Dijkstra/A* implementations, comparison of at least two methods,
a configuration-sampling experiment, trajectory checks, BT and FSM logs, and recovery scenarios.
Foothold planning, hexapod kinodynamics and contact optimisation belong to later specialisation.
Explain now why a circular body footprint and a free floor cell do not establish that a particular
multilegged step is feasible.

75 hours: from the map to controlled
execution

Four work blocks
1-20: graphs, BFS, Dijkstra, A*, heuristics and costmaps; 10 hours of theory and analysis +10 of
practice. 21-40: configuration space, collision checking, PRM/RRT/RRT* and the MoveIt scene;
10+10. 41-60: time parameterisation, splines, velocity, acceleration and jerk limits; 10+10. 61-75:
FSM, BT, recovery, timeouts and MDP/POMDP foundations; 5+10. The total budget is 35 hours of
analysis and 40 hours of practice.
Use 2-hour sessions for the first three theory blocks; divide the last as 2+2+1. Sunday practical
blocks of 10 hours include implementation, measurement and reporting; breaks do not count as
study hours. The numbering below specifies study-hour order, not calendar dates.
Module-boundary dates may overlap; holidays, rest and separate checkpoints remain in the original
calendar without counting hours twice.

Repository structure
Store synthetic and input maps in maps/; algorithms and the common interface in search/; OMPL
settings in sampling/; MoveIt objects and robot parameters in scenes/; q,t files and limits in
trajectories/; the tree, FSM and adapters in autonomy/; and scenarios, seeds and results in
benchmark/. Tie every result to a map version and body footprint. After an obstacle moves, do not
silently continue using the old geometry.

One hypothesis per experiment
To test "A* expands fewer vertices", retain the same graph, cost and tie-breaking order. To test
"RRT* improves the path", compare time and cost under the same budget, including failed runs
with different seeds. To test "smoothing improves motion", recheck collisions: a spline can cut a
corner through a wall. First state the reason for the comparison, then choose the metric.
Finish GUI work by saving a machine-readable scene and launch commands. Do not rely only on
dragging a goal manually in RViz. Prepare a small reference example for each task that another
person can check without the complete setup: a known optimal path, a segment through an
obstacle, a deliberately over-fast trajectory or an expected state trace.

Graph search and the cost of a body route

What to study
BFS finds the shortest path by edge count when edges have equal cost; Dijkstra handles
nonnegative costs; A* adds a heuristic estimate h of cost to the goal. For optimality, study
admissibility and consistency of h, vertex reopening and termination criteria. Manhattan distance is
suitable for a 4-connected grid with unit steps; recheck admissibility for different motions and
costs.
A costmap stores assigned movement costs, while an occupancy map stores occupied, free and
unknown cells. For a finite-size body, account for its footprint, or inflate obstacles by the
circular-model radius with a justified margin. Clearance must account for geometry and
uncertainty. Explicitly define unknown handling: absence of observations does not permit motion.

Task 1. Dijkstra and A* on a robot map
Create an open field, a U-shaped trap and two corridors with different costs. Implement a common
solve(start, goal, map) interface, path reconstruction and an expanded-vertex counter. Output:
path, cost, runtime, expansions and no_path status. Test start=goal, a goal inside an obstacle, an
unreachable goal and equal A*/Dijkstra cost with admissible h. Use BFS as a reference only for
equal edge costs.

Task 2. A narrow passage and a dynamic obstacle
Use a circular body model and a passage around a box. Build two costmaps for different footprint
sizes and compare passage availability. After planning, add an obstacle in the middle of the route.
Mark the old path stale, stop the simulated execution process and replan. Save map and plan
versions, the plan-invalidation log, and the new path or a correct rejection.
Check every segment against the current map; a diagonal must not pass between touching corners
of two occupied cells. Save a case where the body centre passes but the footprint hits a wall.
Explain the future distinction for a leg: the body-centre route does not define a contact sequence,
and height variation requires another state model. In M16, demonstrate this boundary and do not
call a 2D path a complete walking plan.

Sampling-based search and the manipulator
scene

Configuration space
Configuration q contains joint angles, body position or other system coordinates. A workspace
obstacle becomes a set of forbidden q values. Checking one endpoint does not establish the
validity of motion between points. Study joint limits, self-collision and collisions with the
environment and attached object. The configuration-space (C-space) metric must account for units
and periodic angles.
PRM builds a configuration network for repeated queries; RRT grows a tree; RRT* improves
connections to reduce cost as sampling grows. Probabilistic completeness and asymptotic
optimality hold under assumptions and a limiting budget; they do not guarantee a solution within
two seconds. A narrow passage may rarely be sampled. Post-search path simplification also
changes the result and requires checking.

Task 3. Compare sampling-based search
Start with a 2D point or disc model among rectangular obstacles in OMPL. Compare RRTConnect
with RRT* or PRM using equal time per query: at least 10 random-number seeds for three scenes.
Save the exact-solution fraction, time to first solution, length and minimum clearance; account for
PRM construction cost separately. Prepare configs, seed-list and a table of every run, including
timeouts. A missing solution must not be counted as a zero-length path.

Task 4. A MoveIt planning scene
Use a ready-made arm, table and box. Specify object dimensions, positions and orientations in one
coordinate frame; specify start and goal, and plan an approach in simulation. Then attach the
object to the gripper and replan. Save the scene, a valid path and a failure example. Check that the
attached object participates in collision checks and that its frame and scale are correct.
Separately check the path with sufficiently fine discretisation and compare with a coarse check: a
thin obstacle between waypoints must be detected. Document the limits of discrete checking;
without continuous collision checking, it is not a mathematical proof of no contact along the entire
continuous path. The future arm uses this setup to approach an object, while a future leg uses
similar checks for link motion.

Trajectories: time, smoothness and actuator
limits

Core topics
Study linear interpolation, cubic and quintic profiles, and continuity of position, velocity and
acceleration. Jerk is the derivative of acceleration, in rad/s³ for a revolute joint. A geometric spline
may be smooth in parameter s but become infeasible after incorrect time parameterisation. Even
within velocity limits, a trajectory may require excessive torque; distinguish dynamic constraints
explicitly.
For a simple motion from q0 to q1, use q(t)=q0+(q1−q0)·s(t/T), where s(τ)=10τ³−15τ⁴+6τ⁵,
0≤τ≤1. Velocity and acceleration are zero at both endpoints. Study the derivatives: velocity scales
as 1/T, acceleration as 1/T² and jerk as 1/T³. This gives an understandable way to slow an
educational movement, but does not automatically check collisions or dynamics.

Task 5. A leg or finger joint profile
Specify q0,q1 and teaching limits in the configuration, explicitly labelling them as simulation
parameters. Calculate the profile for three T values, plot q,v,a,j and find the smallest tested T that
satisfies the limits with a margin. Output: trajectory.csv, plots, assumptions and a validator. Test
q0=q1, T≤0, non-numeric inputs and an excessively short movement. For a real actuator, obtain
limits from specifications and load tests.

Task 6. Check a path after smoothing
Take the Task 4 path, apply time parameterisation with an available MoveIt tool, and check the
complete result: joint limits, strictly increasing times, derivatives and collisions. Establish whether
the chosen library changes geometry during processing. Output: original and processed paths,
times and rejection reasons. Construct a corner near an obstacle where smooth interpolation
intersects it.
For an introduction to trajectory optimisation, focus on the formulation: variables are states and
controls; constraints are dynamics and obstacles; the objective is time, energy or smoothness.
Solve a small scalar example or study a worked solution without building a full optimiser. Selecting
path topology and continuously optimising a trajectory solve different parts of the overall task;
both outputs require checking.

Autonomous behaviour: states and recovery

Minimal task logic
Define idle, planning, executing, waiting, recovering, failed and completed states. For each, specify
inputs, actions, exit conditions and permitted cancellation. An FSM supports explicit transition
accounting; a behaviour tree supports reusable conditions and actions returning SUCCESS, FAILURE
and RUNNING. Study Sequence, Fallback, reactive condition checks and interruption of an
asynchronous action.
Recovery needs a cause and an attempt limit. For a stale map, refresh observations and replan;
endless retries do not make an unreachable goal reachable. Cancelling an active ROS action
requires acknowledgement and an unambiguous state, otherwise motion may continue after the
goal changes. Measure local timeouts with a monotonic timer; describe simulation time and
recording replay separately.

Task 7. A behaviour tree for a route
Build "check localisation → plan path → follow path", with a branch that waits for fresh data and
bounded replanning. First implement deterministic action stubs, then connect a Nav2 action in
simulation. Output: BT XML or code, a transition table and an event log. Test planner FAILURE,
endless RUNNING and user cancellation; configuration must bound the number of attempts.

Task 8. Measure recovery time
Block the path in simulation, then clear it; measure detection time, time to a new plan and time
until motion resumes. For a permanent obstacle, expect failed or waiting for the operator, rather
than a false completed. Output: a recording, BT settings, events and two plots. Do not transfer
commands from the wheeled Nav2 example directly to a walking body: it needs a separate
executor with contact checks.

MDP and POMDP through a concrete example
Describe a choice between a short unknown section, a detour and an inspection using state, action,
transition and reward. In a POMDP, the true state is hidden; decisions use a belief distribution
updated from observations. Tabulate two traversability hypotheses and update their probabilities
after a camera observation. Reinforcement learning is not required here. Account for map
uncertainty before permitting motion.

Integration setup: plans have a validity
period

Request and plan requirements
Include request_id, current starting position and orientation, goal, frame_id, map_version, robot
model and limits in the request. The response contains exact/approximate/no_path/timeout status,
the path, its validity conditions and checking parameters. approximate status does not establish
goal attainment. Before accepting a trajectory, the executor checks map version, localisation
availability and whether the initial state is current.

Task 9. Connect search, trajectory and behaviour
1. Validate inputs. 2. Calculate an A* or MoveIt path for your setup. 3. Independently check
collisions. 4. Set motion timing and check limits. 5. Send the trajectory to the simulation executor.
6. Monitor map and status changes. 7. On a violation, block acceptance of the next segment,
cancel the action and move the BT into recovery. 8. Save the final state and its cause.
For the body route, use a path between two map points. For the arm, approach a pregrasp pose
and return to the initial state; the grasp cycle itself belongs to M17. A common validator checks
shared data requirements, but the setups have different geometry. Do not present the same point
list as a universal executor for wheels, legs and manipulators.

Task 10. Reproduce an asynchronous fault
Create this situation: plan A is delayed, the user has already requested B, and the old successful
response A then arrives. The executor must reject A using request_id and version, and the log must
show why. Repeat with delayed cancel acknowledgement and completion of the old action. Output:
a reproducible GUI-free test and an event sequence. This is an asynchronous race in robotics: a
mathematically correct path may belong to a task that is no longer current.

Final package contents
Save comparative tests under identical conditions with every seed, MoveIt and Nav2 scenes, path
and trajectory checks, the tree and FSM table, eight failure scenarios, and a README with
commands, versions and limitations. Two short videos must show normal execution and correct
recovery. Measure time from event logs rather than edited video. This software setup does not
validate the physical safety of an actuator or real workspace.

Scenarios the planner must handle

Geometry and data
S1 - start or goal inside an obstacle: return invalid_request before search and do not begin
execution. S2 - A passage narrower than the body footprint: a point path may exist, but reject the
body plan. Check the narrowest point by hand. S3 - A thin wall between waypoints: independent
collision checking must find the intersection; compare two checking resolutions.
S4 - An unknown strip across the map: forbid mode produces a detour or failure; allow is permitted
only as an explicitly labelled teaching experiment. Save both settings without silently changing
them after failure. S5 - A new obstacle during motion: cancel the old plan, increment version and
use the current scene for the new plan. Measure detection time and record the FSM response.

Timing and logic
S6 - An infeasible trajectory: shorten its duration until velocity or acceleration limits are violated.
The check must reject the result and identify the segment and measured quantity. Separately add
non-monotonic timestamps. S7 - Timeout and a stuck action: a stub returns RUNNING beyond the
limit. The tree must bound recovery attempts and finish with an understandable status. Record
attempts and waiting time even if a path is eventually found.
S8 - Cancellation and a late response: request B replaces A, then result A arrives. No segment of A
may be accepted after the change of current request is confirmed. Also test M15 localisation loss:
block new global goals, and do not mark an ended action successful merely because updates
stopped.

Metrics and comparison rules
For search, measure expansions, the number of expanded vertices; cost, the sum of specified edge
costs; and runtime, the duration of the selected code segment. For sampling methods, measure
exact-solution fraction, time to first solution, final cost, minimum clearance and number of collision
checks. Report timeout separately; show median time for successful runs together with success
fraction.
For the autonomous task, measure success fraction, recovery count, time to detect a violation and
build a new plan, and rejected stale-command count. State clocks, tick frequency and hardware.
Body-path length is the sum of segment lengths in metres. For a joint path, the sum of
angular-increment norms means something different: specify joint weights and do not compare it
with metres. Minimum clearance is calculated between geometric bodies at checked
configurations; state discretisation and units. A map may penalise wall proximity, so the shortest
path need not have the lowest cost. No collisions in a finite simulation set describes that set; it
does not prove their absence for every input, parameter and physical contact.

Acceptance: correct rejection is also a result

Required verification results
Compare at least two planners under one protocol; verify Dijkstra/A* cost on reference graphs. In
the sampling experiment, use multiple seeds, count timeouts and check the resulting path. The
MoveIt scene must include an obstacle and an attached object. Reject an infeasible trajectory with
a specific reason; after smoothing, recheck collisions and limits.
The behaviour tree completes the task in a normal scene, recovers from a temporary obstacle and
ends its attempts when the goal remains unreachable. Cancellation works during RUNNING, and a
late result does not replace the current task. Logs connect map_version, request_id, plan and
execution. All eight scenarios are reproducible; launch instructions do not depend on the author's
manual GUI actions.

Self-check
1. When do BFS and Dijkstra produce the same cost? 2. How does an incorrect h remove A*'s
expected optimality? 3. Why does footprint size affect whether a path can be found? 4. How does
PRM usage differ from RRT? 5. Why does RRT* not promise an optimal path in fixed time? 6. What
does checking only waypoints miss?
7. How do v,a,j change when T doubles? 8. Why can a smooth spline collide with a wall? 9. How do
velocity constraints differ from torque constraints? 10. Why is RUNNING neither success nor failure?
11. How do you cancel an old task and reject its late response? 12. What additional information
does a POMDP require compared with a fully observed state?

Handover to manipulation and legs
Pass the arm scene, collision objects, pregrasp path, verified executor, limits and event log to M17.
For later walking, retain the map with unknown, footprint assumptions and constraints absent from
the 2D route. Contact selection, stability, foot reachability and body dynamics will require a new
model. Cancellation and task-freshness checks remain applicable.
The final demonstration must include no_path, an excessively fast trajectory and recovery, as well
as a successful route. Explain the precise level of assurance your test suite provides. "No valid plan
found" is more useful than moving on an uncertain result. Do not claim physical safety of a real
system based on one month of simulation.

Assigned reading: a focused route through
the sections

The literature is mainly in English; the programme and definitions are also in English. Read the
selected sections before the relevant task, rather than entire books. Core reading is included in the
existing 35 analysis hours; consult references as needed.

Core reading
S. LaValle, Planning Algorithms (2006). Author's online book. Ch. 2 Discrete Planning - graph
search; Ch. 4 The Configuration Space - C-space obstacles; Ch. 5 Sampling-Based Motion Planning -
PRM/RRT. Before Tasks 1-4, write out the formulation, validity criterion and termination condition.
K. Lynch, F. Park, Modern Robotics (2017). Book and learning resources. §9.1-9.2
Point-to-Point Trajectories, §9.3 Polynomial Via Point Trajectories, §9.4 Time-Optimal Time Scaling.
In Tasks 5-6, distinguish q(s) and q(t), calculate velocity and acceleration, and check limits.

Task-management reference reading
M. Colledanchise, P. Ögren, Behavior Trees in Robotics and AI: An Introduction. Authors'
book version. §1.3 Classical Formulation of BTs, §2.1 Finite State Machines; in Ch. 3 Design
principles, §3.1-3.2 on success conditions and reactivity. For Tasks 7-8, draw a tree for planning,
following and recovery; check RUNNING returns, stopping and condition reevaluation.

Three implementation references
OMPL: How to Benchmark Planners - Writing benchmarking code and Processing the benchmark
log. In Task 3, save exact/approximate, timeout, run count and validity of the solution found.
MoveIt: Planning Scene - Collision checking, Constraints, State validity. In Task 4, include
self-collision and environment collisions; check a changed scene before execution.
Nav2: Detailed Behavior Tree Walkthrough - Navigation Subtree and Recovery Subtree. For Tasks
7-8: a minimal planning, following and recovery sequence; check XML and plugins against Jazzy.
MDP/POMDP are currently studied only through the distinction between state and observation.
After reading, explain why an exact path in a model can be unexecutable and why timeout failure
must remain visible in comparative test results.

Terms and definitions

Configuration q. A set of independent coordinates defining the robot's geometric arrangement;
velocities usually belong to an expanded state.
Configuration space (C-space). The space of all robot configurations; collisions between
geometric bodies define a forbidden region in this space.
Footprint. The planar projection of the area occupied by the robot, used in mobile-base planning
and passage checks.
Costmap. A discrete representation of obstacles and movement penalties; a cell's cost need not
be its occupancy probability.
Geometric path q(s). A configuration sequence parameterised by progress s; time, velocity and
acceleration are not yet specified.
Trajectory q(t). Motion specified over time, allowing calculation of velocities, accelerations and
actuator requirements.
Dijkstra. Shortest-path search with nonnegative edge costs, processing the smallest current cost
successively.
A*. Search selecting a node by the sum of accumulated cost g and the estimated remaining cost h.
Admissible heuristic. A remaining-cost estimate that never exceeds the true optimum; search
guarantees also depend on implementation and graph conditions.
PRM. A roadmap method using valid random configurations and checked connections between
neighbouring samples.
RRT. A search tree extended towards samples in the space; the basic algorithm aims to find a path
rather than optimise it.
Probabilistic completeness. A property in which the probability of finding an existing solution
approaches one as resources increase and assumptions hold.
Collision checking. Checking robot geometry for intersection with itself and the world; sparse
point checks may miss collisions between them.
State validity. Satisfaction of specified configuration, scene and task constraints; it does not
establish validity of an entire connecting trajectory.
Time parameterisation. Assignment of path progress as a function of time, accounting for
velocity, acceleration and other constraints.
Jerk. The time derivative of acceleration, expressed in m/s³ or rad/s³; describes how abruptly the
applied motion changes.
Behaviour tree (BT). A hierarchical structure executing actions and conditions that return
success, failure or ongoing-execution status.
Tick. A signal to evaluate a tree node; tick frequency determines the timeliness of condition
checking and action cancellation.
FSM. A finite state machine with explicit states and transitions driven by events or conditions;
defines permissible behaviour order.
Recovery. A bounded procedure following a detected failure, with a completion condition, retry
limit and a route to safe stopping.

Motion fundamentals: timing along a path

Required: why geometry is insufficient
The same path can be traversed slowly or abruptly. Configuration geometry determines collisions,
while velocity, acceleration and required torques also depend on time. Prerequisite: take one
revolute leg or finger joint, with q in radians, and explain q̇ and q̈ in rad/s and rad/s².

Two functions rather than one point list
A path q(s), 0≤s≤1, specifies configuration order; a monotonic s(t) specifies progress. The chain
rule gives q̇=q′(s)ṡ and q̈=q″(s)ṡ²+q′(s)s̈. The first acceleration term comes from path curvature
and the second from changes in the rate of progress. Thus smoothness of q(s) does not replace
checks of time derivatives.
For a straight joint-space path q(s)=q0+Δq·s, choose s(t)=3ξ²−2ξ³, ξ=t/T. Then q̇=(Δq/T)(6ξ−6ξ²),
q̈=(Δq/T²)(6−12ξ). Velocity is zero at both endpoints; acceleration there is nonzero and jumps
when transitioning to rest. The quintic profile in the existing Task 5 adds zero boundary
accelerations.

Numerical example and duration selection
Let q0=0, Δq=0.6 rad and T=2 s. Maximum velocity at t=1 s is 1.5Δq/T=0.45 rad/s. Maximum |q̈| at
the endpoints is 6Δq/T²=0.90 rad/s². With example limits of 0.30 rad/s and 0.60 rad/s², this profile
is too fast.
The velocity limit gives T≥1.5·0.6/0.30=3 s; the acceleration limit gives T≥√(6·0.6/0.60)=√6≈2.45
s. Thus this cubic profile requires T≥3 s. At T=3 s, the maxima are 0.30 rad/s and 0.40 rad/s²;
choose a larger T for a margin. This result applies to the chosen profile shape, not the globally
shortest time for any motion.
Foot velocity equals J_p(q)q̇ and varies with configuration; one joint limit does not specify constant
foot speed. Moreover, the torque needed to support weight does not disappear with slow motion.
After checking velocity and acceleration constraints, check dynamics, contacts and geometry;
calculating torque does not set the control mode of a position-controlled servo.

Two exercises in Tasks 5-6
1. Save q,q̇,q̈ for T=2,3,4 s in trajectory.csv. Compare analytical extrema with a dense grid and
numerical derivatives; check q(0),q(T), zero velocities and rejection of T≤0. Then use the same
validator on the Task 5 quintic profile: its maximum coefficients differ.
2. Take the arm path near the box: a monotonic change of time parameterisation alone preserves
path geometry, while waypoint-based path smoothing may change it. Show both versions and
repeated collision checking; include one correct rejection and a plot of the violated constraint.
Time accounting: 1 hour from 41-42 for the derivation; 1 hour each from 51-52 and 53-54 for the
numerical example and validator. Geometry checking remains within the existing 57-58. The
budget stays at 75 hours, with no additional setup required.
Assigned reading: Lynch/Park, Modern Robotics §9.1-9.2, part 1 - path and time; part 2 - cubic
and quintic time scaling and boundary conditions.

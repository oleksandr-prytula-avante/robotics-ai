Uncertainty: how much to trust a sensor

M3 • 29 Nov 2026 - 27 Dec 2026 • 75 hours

This month's deliverable
In M02, you obtained an angle, force or position as a number. M03 adds variability, error sources
and conditions under which a conclusion remains valid. Deliver a portfolio of uncertainty and
optimisation work: a 30-repeat measurement experiment, encoder and IMU noise models,
calibration through optimisation, poor-conditioning diagnostics and a report on link-endpoint
position uncertainty.
This month's questions are practical: does encoder jitter differ from actual motion; how should two
angle estimators be compared; how much independent information does a long recording contain;
why did an optimiser produce a plausible but incorrect scale; how far does joint error move a foot
or fingertip? Do not yet build a Kalman filter, machine-learning model or QP controller: first learn to
check the assumptions they will require.

Shared object and data
Continue with the M02 link L=0.12 m, p(q)=(L cos q,L sin q) and calibration q=a·u+b. Create
data/synthetic, data/measured, experiments, calibration, uncertainty and report. In the data
manifest, record each file's source, units, random-number generator seed, date, independent-run
identifier and how ground truth was obtained. Keep synthetic ground truth separate from algorithm
inputs.
The physical experiment needs no actuator: place a stationary IMU in the same fixture thirty times.
An existing phone that exports raw readings also works. If no sensor is available, first practise the
procedure with synthetic data; the real-measurement section remains incomplete until the
experiment is done. Generated noise does not replace measured device repeatability.

Prerequisites and scope
You need array means, matrices, least squares, derivatives and the meaning of the Jacobian from
M02. In the first two hours, explain the differences between true value, measurement, model
residual and numerical error. Review why the calculated 'velocity' of a stationary encoder can
fluctuate. M03 adds ways to describe these fluctuations and assess the robustness of conclusions.
Begin with definitions: a random variable models possible outcomes; a sample is the data actually
obtained; a parameter is an unknown distribution characteristic; an estimator is a rule for
computing from a sample. One distribution plot does not prove normality, and optimiser success
does not establish physically correct calibration. Both distinctions must appear in your checks.

75 hours: experiments, analysis and
reproducibility

Four work blocks
A, hours 1-20: 10 h theory + 10 h practice. Random variables, distributions, expectation,
variance, covariance, correlation, independence, conditional probability and Bayes' rule. Practical
deliverables: encoder quantisation and IMU noise models, and diagnosis involving a rare event.
B, hours 21-40: 10+10 h. Sampling plans, confidence intervals, effect size, outliers, robust
summaries, hypotheses, repeatability and data leakage. Deliverables: a 30-repeat protocol, raw
recordings and a comparison of two estimators across independent runs.
C, hours 41-60: 10+10 h. Objective functions, constraints, gradients, Hessian meaning, gradient
descent, line search, local minima, least squares and regularisation. Deliverables: calibration with
scipy.optimize, held-out validation and residual analysis.
D, hours 61-75: 5+10 h. SVD, pseudoinverse, poor conditioning, covariance propagation through
a Jacobian, Monte Carlo and bootstrap. Deliverable: an endpoint uncertainty region and checks of
linear-approximation limits. Total: 35 h of theory and analysis plus 40 h of practice.

Organising short and long sessions
Divide each 10 h of theory into five two-hour sessions and the final 5 h into 2+2+1. Each 2 h
session should cover one concept, one hand-worked example and one decision about the
forthcoming experiment. Schedule 10 h practical blocks in available Sunday or holiday sessions
from the original plan; divide each into five 2 h segments and count breaks separately.
The calendar window follows the original plan. A boundary date may coincide with a neighbouring
module; holidays, rest and integration stages are preserved. Allocate step hours sequentially to
available sessions, without double counting or adding another 'compensation' hour.

Experiment log
Before collecting data, record the question, primary metric, unit of independent repetition,
recording-exclusion criterion and stopping rule. Afterwards, add actual deviations from the
protocol. Do not delete an inconvenient recording because it worsens the mean: retain it with a
reason and, if necessary, present analysis with and without exclusion.
If time is short, remove optional CVXPY comparison and complex interactive plots. Retain 30
repeats, basic calibration, splitting data by run, SVD diagnostics and Monte Carlo calculation.
Another book cannot replace the measurement work. Being able to say 'these data do not yet
establish the claim' is a valid laboratory outcome.

Probability and sensor-noise models

Topics to study
Study discrete and continuous random variables, interval probabilities, probability density and
cumulative distribution functions. For sensors, use a normal distribution as a working model of
small disturbances, a uniform distribution as a quantisation simplification, and a Bernoulli
distribution for 'packet lost'. Expectation describes the centre; variance is the mean squared
deviation; standard deviation has the same units as the measurement.
Covariance describes joint variation of two channels; correlation normalises it by their standard
deviations. Zero correlation alone does not establish independence. Consecutive stationary-IMU
samples can be dependent because of internal filters and drift. A thousand samples therefore need
not equal a thousand independent experiment repeats.

Task 1. Encoder quantisation
Input: true angle q within −0.5…0.5 rad and quantisation step Δ=0.001 rad. Compute rounded
q_meas, error e=q_meas−q, a histogram and empirical cumulative distribution function. For
uniform coverage of quantisation intervals, compare variance with Δ²/12. Then take a stationary
angle exactly between two codes and explain why this distribution model need no longer describe
the observations.
Deliverables: generator, two different input trajectories and a table of mean, standard deviation
(SD) and maximum error. Check |e|≤Δ/2 under the chosen rounding convention, apart from stated
numerical tolerances. Failure case: the model saturates beyond range; an error does not become
uniform merely because the data came from an encoder.

Task 2. Shared noise on two IMU axes
Generate 5000 synthetic error pairs by adding each axis's independent noise to a shared
component. Record the covariance matrix and explain its units: for acceleration, (m/s²)². Plot the
point cloud and compare it with independent data having the same per-channel variance. Equal
axis standard deviations do not imply equal uncertainty in their sum.
Check covariance symmetry and nonnegative eigenvalues, allowing for rounding. Add slow shared
drift and compare the recording's first and second halves. Deliver three point clouds and explain
the effect shown by each. The aim is to recognise when constant-noise assumptions fail, rather
than force a 'correct' distribution onto the data.

Conditional probability and diagnostic
reliability

Bayes' rule for a clear robot event
Study P(A|B), the law of total probability and Bayes' rule using a frequency table. Example: A is a
genuine foot-contact failure; B is a threshold diagnostic trigger. Sensitivity P(B|A) and the
probability of a real failure after a trigger P(A|B) answer different questions. The latter also depends
on event prevalence.
For the teaching example, use failure probability 1%, sensitivity 95% and a 5% false-trigger
probability without failure. These are illustrative calculation values, not properties of an actual
sensor. Among 10 000 virtual episodes, expect 95 true and 495 false triggers; about 16.1% of all
alerts are true. Check using a probability tree or table.

Task 3. Rare events and thresholds
Create an episode generator with a known label and diagnostic feature. Calculate TP, FP, FN, TN,
then precision=TP/(TP+FP) and recall=TP/(TP+FN). Show how both metrics and trigger frequency
change across several thresholds. If a denominator is zero, return 'undefined' rather than silently
assign a perfect score.
Repeat with event probabilities 0.1%, 1% and 10%, retaining the feature's conditional distributions.
Deliver a table and short note identifying the more consequential error type for this test bench.
Here, diagnostics only create log events; this model does not verify a robot's actual protective
functions.

Task 4. Why independence needs checking
Generate white noise and a correlated series e_t=0.95·e_(t−1)+η_t. Scale them to approximately
equal SD; compare time plots and adjacent-sample correlation. Average over equal-length windows
and measure variation of means across non-overlapping windows. Equal sample counts do not
guarantee equal improvement.
Control check: shuffle the correlated series and repeat. Shuffling preserves the marginal
distribution but destroys temporal structure. Save two plots and explain why randomly splitting
rows of one recording can yield an overly optimistic algorithm evaluation. Exact
effective-sample-size theory is optional; the meaning of an independent run must be understood
now.

Statistics: a real 30-repeat experiment

Write the protocol before recording
Task 5 assesses the repeatability of a stationary IMU repositioned in the same fixture. Choose a
device that exports timestamps and raw accelerometer and gyroscope readings; record its settings
and available rate. Perform 30 separate placements, keeping the device still each time, waiting the
same predetermined settling interval and recording for the same duration. Choose a duration
supported by export; the teaching guideline is 2 s after settling.
The repeat unit is one independent run after repositioning, not one CSV row. Record run_id, fixture
orientation, time, temperature if available, observed interference and setting changes. Within each
run, calculate the axis mean and SD; across 30 runs, examine variation of these means.
Repositioning adds geometric uncertainty, so describe the result as repeatability of the whole
procedure.

Calculations and interpretation
For the 30 means, find the mean, median, SD and interquartile range. If assumptions are
reasonable, construct a 95% t-confidence interval for the mean: mean ± t·s/sqrt(n). This interval
concerns the procedure's mean; it is neither a range covering 95% of future individual readings nor
a promise to cover a particular sensor's true angle.
If run independence or stability fails, show a time plot and limit the conclusion. Do not treat 30 as
universally sufficient: justify this pilot sample size using available time and desired interval width,
then estimate the additional independent repeats needed to halve the interval width under the
same assumptions.

Task 6. Outlier robustness and effect size
Compare two constant-level estimates on the same 30 runs: within-recording arithmetic mean and
median. Add labelled individual outliers to copies of several recordings. Calculate the change in
error relative to the clean copy and paired estimate differences, rather than choose an algorithm
from histogram appearance. Express effects in physical units and relate them to the robot task.
Deliverables: protocol.md, 30 raw-data files or one CSV with run_id, a summary table and a plot in
test order. Edge cases: empty run, gaps, changed sampling rate and noticeable device motion.
Define exclusion criteria before viewing results. Without independent ground truth, compare
robustness and repeatability without claiming absolute IMU accuracy.
To introduce hypothesis testing, state H0: the mean paired difference between the two estimates is
zero. Discuss false rejection of H0 and missing a real effect; statistical significance does not say
whether an effect is useful to the robot. Report the difference in physical units and its interval in
the main conclusion, rather than a p-value alone.

Calibration optimisation: from error to
parameters

Minimum theory
A loss function answers a specific question: how far do model predictions differ from
measurements? Parameters θ may include encoder scale and zero; constraints express meaningful
admissible values. The gradient gives the local direction of increasing loss; the Hessian describes
local curvature. Begin with a two-variable quadratic bowl that can be drawn and whose derivatives
can be checked by hand.
Gradient descent updates θ_new=θ−α·grad. Excessive α can increase error; too small an α slows
progress. Learn backtracking line search: reduce the step until the objective decreases by the
required amount. Distinguish stopping criteria based on step, gradient and loss; a small step may
indicate poor scaling rather than a good minimum.

Task 7. Implement descent for scale and zero
Using M02 calibration, scale u to a comparable magnitude and derive the sum-of-squares gradient
with respect to a and b. Check it with central finite differences at five parameter points. Run
descent from three initial guesses with two step strategies; save the loss curve, iteration count and
final coefficients.
On noise-free data, compare with linear lstsq. For poorly scaled u, explain elongated contours and
slow convergence. Deliverables: short implementation, contour plot and table. Edge case: wrong
gradient sign; a test must detect increasing loss and stop the run. Choose the gradient-check
threshold relative to function scale and finite-difference step.

Task 8. Constrained nonlinear model
Use the teaching model q=a·u+b+c·u² with normalised u. Prepare independent calibration and
verification series; generate a small known nonlinear component. Pass the residuals vector, not
total loss, to scipy.optimize.least_squares. Specify bounds, initial guess and parameter scales;
compare ordinary least squares and robust loss with labelled outliers.
Regularise c by adding residual sqrt(λ)·c after choosing a dimensionless scale. Compare several λ
values on the validation set, then evaluate the result once on the test set. Show a case where the
linear model performs better on held-out data despite greater training error. The optimum is an
estimate within the chosen model; still check residual dependence on position and motion
direction.

SVD, pseudoinverses and uncertainty
propagation

Core concepts
SVD factors A=U·Σ·Vᵀ into orthogonal input/output directions and stretch factors. A very small
singular value indicates a parameter direction weakly represented in the data. A pseudoinverse
selects a solution of a singular problem according to a defined rule; it cannot recover information
the experiment never measured.
For p=f(x) and small uncertainties near x̄, use Σ_p≈J·Σ_x·Jᵀ. Σ_x is input covariance; evaluate J
specifically at x̄. Covariance has squared units of the corresponding output quantities. This is a
local approximation, so compare with nonlinear Monte Carlo calculation, especially near geometric
singularities or with large variability.

Task 9. Weakly determined calibration
Take the calibration matrix for a wide u range and the same matrix with inputs restricted to a
narrow band. After applying the same scaling, compare singular values, cond and parameter
changes under a small q perturbation. Find the right singular vector for the smallest singular value:
it identifies the parameter combination least distinguishable in the data.
Save a plot of the uncertain parameter direction and predict which u values a new experiment
should add. Then add extreme positions to the synthetic data and verify improvement. Test: all u
values identical; pinv may return a solution, but the report must identify insufficient rank and
nonunique physical calibration.

Task 10. Foot-position variability
Use L=0.12 m, q=30° and teaching standard uncertainties σ_L=0.0005 m and σ_q=0.005 rad.
Repeat with large noise σ_q=0.3 rad; set nonzero correlation ρ=0.6 while keeping covariance
positive semidefinite. Let x=(L,q), and derive J for p=(L cos q,L sin q). First assume independent
errors, then add admissible nonzero covariance. Compute Σ_p, its eigenvector directions and a 10
000-point Monte Carlo cloud.
Compare coordinate SDs and covariance from the linear prediction with sample estimates. Repeat
for small and large angle noise; record relative discrepancies while avoiding division by nearly zero
covariance. The ellipse visualises the assumed uncertainty model, not a guaranteed real-robot
position region. Negative sampled length requires revising the distribution model rather than
silently deleting inconvenient points.

Integrated laboratory and six validity checks

Assemble the final package
Combine calibration and uncertainty: input encoder_count passes through the chosen q(u,θ) model,
then geometry p(L,q). For each validation run, save predicted position, estimated variability and
actual error if independent ground truth exists. Synthetic experiments have known ground truth;
the real stationary-IMU experiment usually establishes only repeatability.
Create three scenarios: nominal noise, correlated drift and outliers. Split independent series into
training (train), validation and test sets. Choose the model, λ and threshold using train and
validation; open test only after fixing settings. If you subsequently change the solution, mark the
test set as used and prepare a new independent check: repeated tuning removes test
independence.

Required error checks
1. Leakage. Compare random row splits with whole-run splits under slow drift; show why the
former appears better. 2. Pseudoreplication. Duplicate each row 100 times; the reporting
algorithm must not count these as new independent experiments. 3. Outlier. Add one large spike
and compare mean, median and robust loss without deleting the original.
4. Singularity. Nearly constant u must trigger a weak-identifiability warning regardless of solver
success. 5. Nonlinearity. Large q noise invalidates linear covariance propagation; Monte Carlo
must reveal the discrepancy. 6. Nonstationarity. Different means at the start and end require a
separate drift discussion rather than one 'sensor σ'.

Bootstrap: an additional interval check
Resample with replacement from the 30 independent run results and estimate intervals for the
median or paired difference. Record resample count and seed; repeat with another seed to observe
computational variation. Do not resample correlated individual rows as independent observations.
Temporal dependence requires another scheme, such as block bootstrap, which is sufficient to
identify as a later topic.
Distinguish observation and interpretation on every report page. A useful format is: 'between-run
SD is … under these conditions; it includes repositioning; no independent absolute reference is
available'. Do not fill numerical fields in advance. Save metrics with units, interval bounds,
independent-repeat count and specific applicability limits.

Session sequence: hours 1-40

Block A - build a noise model and identify its limits
Hours 1-2. Review error, true value and estimator definitions; prepare the data structure. Hours
3-4. Study distributions, expectation and variance; calculate probabilities of three discrete-encoder
outcomes by hand. Hours 5-6. Study covariance and correlation with two channels. Hours 7-8.
Study conditional probability and Bayes' rule through a diagnostic table. Hours 9-10. Study
independence, temporal dependence and the experimental unit.
Hours 11-20 - practice. 2 h: quantisation and Δ/2 check. 2 h: independent and shared IMU noise.
2 h: rare event, TP/FP/FN/TN. 2 h: white and correlated series. 2 h: plots, generator tests and
conclusions. For each plot, state the sensor assumption built into the generator and a real effect it
does not yet describe.
Hour 20 checkpoint: explain to someone without statistics training why high diagnostic sensitivity
can coexist with many false alarms, and why 5000 adjacent samples do not equal 5000
repositionings. If you cannot, revisit the frequency table and temporal-dependence plot.

Block B - make a justified comparison
Hours 21-22. Study sampling and estimation; choose the measured quantity. Hours 23-24. Write
the 30-repeat protocol and exclusion criteria. Hours 25-26. Study SD, standard error and a
t-confidence interval for the mean. Hours 27-28. Study outliers, median, IQR and effect size.
Hours 29-30. Study training, validation and test sets, paired comparison and repeatability.
Hours 31-40 - practice. 2 h: fixture preparation and pilot export; 2 h: 30 measurement runs with
metadata; 2 h: integrity checks and individual-run summaries; 2 h: intervals and estimator
comparison; 2 h: report and excluded-recording review. If longer recordings are necessary, reduce
styling and use prepared summary scripts while retaining the independent-repeat count.
Checkpoint: another person can understand the dataset's experiment boundaries, what remained
stationary, the coordinate frame used and why some recordings are marked doubtful. Without this
information, measurements cannot support rigorous calibration comparisons in later months.

Session sequence: hours 41-75

Block C - optimise and verify calibration
Hours 41-42. Write the loss function for q=a·u+b and check dimensions. Hours 43-44. Derive
and numerically check the gradient; draw curvature in two parameters. Hours 45-46. Study fixed
steps and backtracking. Hours 47-48. Study nonlinear-model residuals, constraints and initial
guesses. Hours 49-50. Study regularisation and model selection using validation data.
Hours 51-60 - practice. 2 h: implement descent and compare with lstsq; 2 h: least_squares with
bounds; 2 h: outliers and robust losses; 2 h: several λ values and independent series; 2 h: gradient
checks, model saving and residual analysis. For every run, retain status, evaluation count, initial
guess, objective value and physical coefficients.
In the last two-hour segment, deliberately create a problem with local minima, such as periodic
zero-offset ambiguity in sin(u+b), and start from different b values. This briefly introduces local
minima and period ambiguity; do not add this complexity to the main calibrator. Conclude that
results depend on the model and observational information, not merely the algorithm's name.

Block D - uncertainty in the result
Hours 61-62. Study SVD geometry and truncation of small singular values; check a diagonal
example by hand. Hours 63-64. Derive J with respect to L and q, and propagate Σ. Hour 65. Study
Monte Carlo, bootstrap, and measurement uncertainty versus computational variation in an
estimate.
Hours 66-75 - practice. 2 h: poor calibration, SVD and a revised measurement plan. 2 h:
analytical endpoint covariance. 2 h: Monte Carlo and large noise. 2 h: bootstrap by run and
consolidated report. 2 h: six error checks, a fresh run and defending conclusions.
Final half-hour task without copying: specify two noise models with equal SD but different
cross-axis dependence. Before running, predict which gives greater variation in a chosen
foot-position projection. Check using the covariance formula and simulation. If the prediction was
wrong, correct the explanation and preserve the mistake as a useful finding.
Do not measure success by method count. The required minimum is one explainable calibrator,
one real experiment with justified conclusions, one poor-identifiability example and one
error-propagation check. Rust/C++ may read M01 logs, but reimplementing all statistics in three
languages is outside the 75 h.

Laboratory acceptance and the future robot

Verifiable deliverables
The package must include ten tasks with initial conditions, a 30-repeat protocol, labelled real and
synthetic data, distribution models with limitations, calibration configurations, splits by series,
residual plots, SVD diagnostics, covariance and Monte Carlo calculations. Record commands,
library versions and report-reproduction order in README.
Every substantial number must answer: 'which data?', 'what unit of repetition?', 'what physical
units?' and 'which metric?'. A mean without run count or an interval without a calculation method
fails acceptance. Do not report more significant figures than uncertainty supports; preserve full
machine precision separately for reproduction.

Check your understanding
Explain variance versus SD; independence versus zero correlation; random spread versus
systematic bias; repeatability versus absolute accuracy. Use a table to distinguish P(failure|alarm)
from P(alarm|failure). Why can you not narrow a confidence interval simply by copying rows?
For optimisation: what goes into residuals; why normalise parameters; why is success insufficient;
what does a small singular value reveal; what does regularisation change, and what does it not
know about the robot? For uncertainty: where is J evaluated; what are Σ's units; when does linear
propagation fail; which units should bootstrap resample?
Practical assessment: take a new independent synthetic series with a seed unknown to you,
calibrate and compare estimates with saved ground truth. Add drift and state which assumptions
fail. For real data, show one supported conclusion and one requiring a different reference or
experiment.

Handover
Pass M04 the actual sampling rates, gaps, noise summaries, original recordings and corrupted
copies for filtering work. Pass M05 length-and-angle error propagation as an example connecting
tolerances to foot position. Later filters and multi-joint calibration can reuse these functions, but a
sensor's numerical covariance must be remeasured after mounting or settings change.
Keep open questions about temperature drift, vibration dependence, axis correlation, reverse
mechanism travel and reference accuracy. For each, describe the smallest future experiment that
could test it. This makes the laboratory a foundation for later hardware tests, beyond a collection of
one-off plots.

Assigned reading: a short route

Read only the specified passages before the corresponding experiment. References are mainly in
English; keep explanations, glossary and report in English. Reading and preparation fit within the
existing 75 hours; whole books are not required.

Core reading
1. Allen B. Downey, Think Stats, 3rd online edition. Chapters 2, 4, 7, 8: Distributions;
Cumulative Distribution Functions; Relationships between variables; Estimation. Tasks 1-6 need
distributions, relationships and estimation uncertainty; adapt the examples to your measurements.
2. NIST/SEMATECH, e-Handbook of Statistical Methods. §1.3.5.2 Confidence Limits for the
Mean: interval from 30 independent runs; §1.3.5.11, skewness and kurtosis: noise-shape reference.
Write interval assumptions before inserting numbers.
3. Stephen Boyd, Lieven Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization (2004). §4.1, §6.1, §6.3,
§9.2-9.3. Problem formulation; norm approximation; regularisation; descent and gradient methods.
In tasks 7-8, connect loss, constraints and parameter scale to physical calibration. General-case
proofs are optional.

Compact implementation references
SciPy optimize. least_squares: fun, jac, bounds, x_scale, loss. Before task 8, check stopping
criteria and output fields. A success status does not replace unit, bound and held-out-data checks.
NumPy. svd: Returns, Notes. For task 9, reconstruct A from U,S,Vh, then compare the
pseudoinverse with pinv. Explain what changes when a small singular value is truncated.
SciPy stats. bootstrap: data, statistic, paired, method. Resample independent runs; use paired
only for paired comparisons. Optional constrained least-squares comparison: CVXPY example.
Reading sequence. First distributions and the 30-repeat plan, then estimates and intervals, then
optimisation and SVD. Deliver one sheet of experiment assumptions and one defining the
calibration problem.

Glossary: terms and definitions

Random variable. A numerical function of a possible random-experiment outcome; its distribution
specifies probabilities of values or ranges.
Independence. Property of events or variables whereby information about one does not change
the probability distribution of the other.
Conditional probability. Probability of A given B: P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B), provided P(B)>0.
Sample. The particular set of observations obtained; under temporal dependence, its size is not
the number of independent observations.
Parameter and estimate. A parameter characterises an unknown model; an estimate
approximates it from a particular sample using a specified rule.
Bias. Difference between an estimator's expectation and the true parameter. Averaging does not
remove constant sensor bias.
Variance. Expected squared deviation from the mean; describes variability and has squared units
of the measured quantity.
Standard deviation. Square root of variance; describes individual-value variability in original
units, such as rad or m/s².
Standard error. Standard deviation of an estimator's distribution over repeated samples;
describes estimation uncertainty rather than individual-reading variability.
Covariance. Mean product of centred deviations of two quantities; its sign describes the direction
of their joint linear variation.
Correlation. Normalised covariance; describes linear association, but zero generally does not
imply independence.
Confidence interval. Interval produced by a procedure that, over repeated experiments, covers a
fixed parameter at the specified frequency.
Bootstrap. Estimation of a statistic's distribution by repeated sampling with replacement. The
resampling unit must match the original experiment's independent unit.
Loss function. Scalar measure of model-data discrepancy; its choice determines which errors
have greater influence on calibration.
Gradient. Vector of a scalar function's partial derivatives; gives the local direction of greatest
increase for the chosen coordinates and metric.
Regularisation. An additional preference or penalty in estimation that limits unstable solutions at
the cost of possible systematic bias.
SVD (singular value decomposition). Factorisation A=UΣVᵀ for a real matrix; singular values
describe amplification along mutually orthogonal directions.
Identifiability. Ability to distinguish parameters from available observations and inputs; repeating
the same experiment can preserve model ambiguity.

Motion fundamentals: how angle error moves
the foot

Compulsory, within 75 hours. Use 1 h from 63-64 and 4 h from block 66-75 already allocated to
analytical covariance and Monte Carlo in task 10. No new measurements or equipment are
required. Entry check: explain zero-offset error, random variability and standard error of the mean.

Displacement direction depends on the error source
Use the task 10 link: L=0.12 m, q=π/6 rad, in a fixed planar frame with x right and y upwards; p=(L
cos q,L sin q). Parameters u=(L,q) have small independent zero-mean errors: σ_L=0.0005 m,
σ_q=0.005 rad. This is a specified teaching model, not a claim about real-encoder accuracy.
A small length error moves the endpoint along the link: ∂p/∂L=(cos q,sin q)ᵀ. Angle error acts
tangentially: ∂p/∂q=(−L sin q,L cos q)ᵀ. Assemble these columns into J to obtain δp≈Jδu. Here u
denotes the parameter pair, not encoder_count in the calibration model.
Covariance transforms as Σ_p≈J·diag(σ_L²,σ_q²)·Jᵀ. Independent input errors do not imply
independent x and y: both outputs depend on L and q. Standard spread along the link is 0.5 mm
and across it Lσ_q=0.6 mm; these are ellipse semiaxes at one standard uncertainty, not a
guaranteed foot-position region.

A calculation you can check by hand
At 30°, Σ_xx=0.2775 mm², Σ_yy=0.3325 mm², Σ_xy=Σ_yx=−0.047631 mm². Thus σ_x≈0.5268 mm
and σ_y≈0.5766 mm. The matrix trace is 0.61 mm², the sum 0.5²+0.6²; rotating axes changes
components but preserves this trace.
Now add a constant zero-offset error Δq=+0.01 rad. The linear mean shift J_qΔq≈(−0.600;1.039)
mm has length 1.2 mm. The exact change p(q+Δq)−p(q)≈(−0.605;1.036) mm. Repeating one
position can reduce uncertainty in the estimated mean, but does not remove this constant
geometric shift.

Verify task 10 and decide what to calibrate
1. Using the same 10 000-point Monte Carlo cloud, compare both variances and covariance with
the calculation, then rotate axes by 90° and check trace and eigenvalues. Save a drawing of the
link, ellipse and cloud. Do not use relative error of nearly zero covariance as the sole metric; allow
for Monte Carlo's own variability.
2. Add Δq separately and show the cloud-centre shift; then restore the task's large σ_q=0.3 rad.
Explain why a tangent describes the arc less accurately. In the report, state what needs correcting:
zero offset, length or random-error model. M03 phone repeatability alone does not calibrate a
future leg's geometry.
Read before calculating: NIST/SEMATECH, §2.5.5: propagation of error, sensitivity coefficients;
Lynch, Park, Modern Robotics, chapter 5 introduction: Jacobian.
